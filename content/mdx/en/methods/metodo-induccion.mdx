<Capitular letra="M" />athematical induction is a deductive proof technique used to establish the universal validity of an infinite family of statements $\{P(n)\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ indexed over the set of <ConceptLink targetId="numero-natural">natural numbers</ConceptLink> $\mathbb{N}$. Axiomatically, it is rooted in the well-ordering principle of $\mathbb{N}$ and Peano's axioms.

<Separador />

### Structure of an Inductive Proof

A proof by mathematical induction consists of two canonical stages:

<ProofStep number={1} target="domino1" diagramStep="paso-caso-base" title="Base Case: P(1)">
  Demonstrating directly that the statement holds for the initial element (typically $n = 1$ or $n = 0$), ensuring the first domino <VisualBind element="domino1" color="terracota">$P(1)$</VisualBind> falls:
  <Formula>
    $$ P(1) \text{ is true} $$
  </Formula>
</ProofStep>

<ProofStep number={2} target="dominok" diagramStep="paso-hipotesis-induccion" title="Inductive Hypothesis: P(k)">
  Assuming as the *induction hypothesis* that the statement holds for an arbitrary positive <ConceptLink targetId="numero-entero">integer</ConceptLink> $k \ge 1$, represented by domino <VisualBind element="dominok" color="ocre">$P(k)$</VisualBind>:
  <Formula>
    $$ P(k) \text{ is assumed true for some } k \ge 1 $$
  </Formula>
</ProofStep>

<ProofStep number={3} target="dominok1" diagramStep="paso-inductivo" title="Inductive Step: P(k) ⇒ P(k+1)">
  From the truth of <VisualBind element="dominok" color="ocre">$P(k)$</VisualBind>, deriving that the statement must also hold for the successor <VisualBind element="dominok1" color="mora">$P(k+1)$</VisualBind>, guaranteeing that each domino topples its neighbor:
  <Formula>
    $$ \forall k \in \mathbb{N}, \quad P(k) \implies P(k+1) $$
  </Formula>
</ProofStep>

<ProofStep number={4} target={["domino1", "domino2", "domino3", "dominok", "dominok1"]} diagramStep="paso-conclusion-universal" title="Universal Conclusion: ∀n ∈ ℕ, P(n)">
  Once both conditions are verified, the principle of mathematical induction establishes that the entire infinite domino chain falls:
  <Formula>
    $$ [P(1) \land (\forall k \ge 1, \, P(k) \implies P(k+1))] \implies \forall n \in \mathbb{N}, \, P(n) $$
  </Formula>
</ProofStep>

<Separador />

### Main Applications

- **Finite Sums and Sequences**: Proving closed-form summations such as the sum of the first $n$ positive integers:
  $$ \sum_{i=1}^n i = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} $$
- **Combinatorial Geometry**: Proving that the sum of the interior <ConceptLink targetId="angulo">angles</ConceptLink> of a simple $n$-<ConceptLink targetId="lado">sided</ConceptLink> <ConceptLink targetId="poligono">polygon</ConceptLink> is $(n-2) \times 180^\circ$, generalizing the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-suma-angulos-triangulo">triangle angle sum theorem</ConceptLink> via successive triangulations.
- **Inequalities and Divisibility**: Validating classical bounds like Bernoulli's inequality $(1+x)^n \ge 1+nx$ for $x > -1$.

<Separador />

### Formal Variations

- **Strong (Complete) Induction**: The induction hypothesis assumes that $P(m)$ holds for all integers $1 \le m \le k$ in order to deduce $P(k+1)$.
- **Structural and Transfinite Induction**: Generalizations of the induction principle applied to recursively defined data structures and transfinite ordinals in axiomatic set theory.
