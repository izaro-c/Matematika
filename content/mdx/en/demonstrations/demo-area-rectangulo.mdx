<DemonstrationSection>

<Capitular letra="D" />irect <ConceptLink targetId="metodo-directo" isDependency={true}>proof</ConceptLink> of the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-area-rectangulo">rectangle area theorem</ConceptLink>: we first prove that the area of a <ConceptLink targetId="rectangulo" isDependency={true}>rectangle</ConceptLink> depends only on the <ConceptLink targetId="longitud" isDependency={true}>lengths</ConceptLink> of its sides and is additive in each of them; from this the formula follows for <ConceptLink targetId="numero-entero">integer</ConceptLink> sides, then for <ConceptLink targetId="numero-racional">rational</ConceptLink> sides and, finally, for <ConceptLink targetId="numero-real">real</ConceptLink> sides by means of the <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-arquimedes" isDependency={true}>Archimedean axiom</ConceptLink>.

<Separador />

### Step-by-step proof

<ProofStep number={1} title="The area depends only on the side lengths">
Let $ABCD$ and $A'B'C'D'$ be two <ConceptLink targetId="rectangulo" isDependency={true}>rectangles</ConceptLink> with $|AB| = |A'B'| = b$ and $|AD| = |A'D'| = h$. The <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo-rectangulo" isDependency={true}>right triangles</ConceptLink> $\triangle DAB$ and $\triangle D'A'B'$ have <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-recto" isDependency={true}>right angles</ConceptLink> at $A$ and $A'$ and congruent legs, so $\triangle DAB \cong \triangle D'A'B'$ by the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-congruencia-lal" isDependency={true}>SAS criterion</ConceptLink>; the same holds for $\triangle BCD$ and $\triangle B'C'D'$. Each rectangle is the union of its two triangles, which have disjoint interiors, so invariance under congruence and additivity of the <ConceptLink targetId="teoria-medida-area" isDependency={true}>area function</ConceptLink> give $\operatorname{A}(ABCD) = \operatorname{A}(A'B'C'D')$. We may therefore write $f(b, h)$ for the area of any rectangle with adjacent sides of lengths $b$ and $h$.
</ProofStep>

<ProofStep number={2} title="Additivity in each side">
Let $b = b_1 + b_2$ with $b_1, b_2 > 0$. By <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-congruencia-1" isDependency={true}>Axiom III.1</ConceptLink> there are $E \in \operatorname{seg}(AB)$ and $F \in \operatorname{seg}(DC)$ with $|AE| = |DF| = b_1$; since $\overline{AE}$ and $\overline{DF}$ are congruent and lie on <ConceptLink targetId="paralelas" isDependency={true}>parallel</ConceptLink> lines, $AEFD$ and $EBCF$ are rectangles (<ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-euclides" isDependency={true}>parallel axiom</ConceptLink>) that only share the <ConceptLink targetId="segmento" isDependency={true}>segment</ConceptLink> $\overline{EF}$. By the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-area-aditividad" isDependency={true}>additivity of area</ConceptLink>, and by the same argument for the other side,

$$
f(b_1 + b_2, h) = f(b_1, h) + f(b_2, h), \qquad f(b, h_1 + h_2) = f(b, h_1) + f(b, h_2)
$$
</ProofStep>

<ProofStep number={3} title="Integer sides">
Applying the previous step repeatedly, for $m, n \in \mathbb{N}$ and $x, y > 0$ we get $f(m x, n y) = m \cdot n \cdot f(x, y)$: the rectangle with sides $m x$ and $n y$ is divided into $m \cdot n$ rectangles with sides $x$ and $y$. With $x = y = 1$ and the normalisation $f(1, 1) = 1$ of the unit <ConceptLink targetId="cuadrado" isDependency={true}>square</ConceptLink>, the rectangle is divided into $m \cdot n$ unit squares and

$$
f(m, n) = m \cdot n
$$
</ProofStep>

<ProofStep number={4} title="Rational sides">
Let $b = p/q$ and $h = m/n$ with $p, q, m, n \in \mathbb{N}$, and let $N = q n$. By the properties of <ConceptLink targetId="longitud" isDependency={true}>length</ConceptLink>, which rest on the axioms of continuity (<ConceptLink targetId="axioma-arquimedes" isDependency={true}>Axiom V.1</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-completitud" isDependency={true}>Axiom V.2</ConceptLink>), there is a segment of length $1/N$. The unit square is divided into $N^2$ squares of side $1/N$, hence $1 = N^2 f(1/N, 1/N)$ and each of them has area $1/N^2$. Since $b = (p n)/N$ and $h = (m q)/N$, the rectangle is divided into $(p n) \cdot (m q)$ squares of side $1/N$, so that

$$
f(b, h) = \frac{(p n)(m q)}{(q n)^2} = \frac{p}{q} \cdot \frac{m}{n} = b \cdot h
$$
</ProofStep>

<ProofStep number={5} title="Monotonicity">
If $0 < b' \leq b$ and $0 < h' \leq h$, by step 2 the rectangle with sides $b, h$ decomposes into the one with sides $b', h'$ and at most two further rectangles; by the positivity of area,

$$
f(b', h') \leq f(b, h)
$$

(this is a case of the <ConceptLink targetId="monotonia-area">monotonicity of area</ConceptLink>).
</ProofStep>

<ProofStep number={6} title="Real sides: approximation from below and from above">
Let $b, h > 0$ be arbitrary and $k \in \mathbb{N}$. By the Archimedean property (<ConceptLink targetId="axioma-arquimedes" isDependency={true}>Archimedean axiom</ConceptLink>), there is a largest integer $j \geq 0$ with $j/k \leq b$, and likewise a largest integer $i \geq 0$ with $i/k \leq h$; let $b_k = j/k$ and $h_k = i/k$, so that $b_k \leq b < b_k + 1/k$ and $h_k \leq h < h_k + 1/k$. For $k$ large enough, $b_k, h_k > 0$. By steps 4 and 5 (the endpoints are rational), $f(b, h)$ and $b \cdot h$ both lie in the interval $[\, b_k h_k,\ (b_k + 1/k)(h_k + 1/k) \,]$, whose length is

$$
\left(b_k + \tfrac{1}{k}\right)\left(h_k + \tfrac{1}{k}\right) - b_k h_k = \frac{b_k + h_k}{k} + \frac{1}{k^2} \leq \frac{b + h + 1}{k}
$$

Thus $|f(b, h) - b \cdot h| \leq (b + h + 1)/k$ for every sufficiently large $k$. If $f(b, h) \neq b \cdot h$, the Archimedean property would give a $k$ with $(b + h + 1)/k < |f(b, h) - b \cdot h|$, which is absurd. Therefore $\operatorname{A}(ABCD) = f(b, h) = b \cdot h$.
</ProofStep>

<Nota>
The last step is a version of the method of exhaustion of <ConceptLink targetId="eudoxo">Eudoxus</ConceptLink>, which <ConceptLink targetId="arquimedes">Archimedes</ConceptLink> used systematically: a magnitude is determined by trapping it between approximations from below and from above whose difference can be made smaller than any given quantity. Without the <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-arquimedes">Archimedean axiom</ConceptLink>, Hilbert still develops the theory of area (chapter IV), but with values in a field of segments rather than in $\mathbb{R}$.
</Nota>
