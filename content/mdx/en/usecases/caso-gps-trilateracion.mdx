<Capitular letra="T" />he Global Positioning System (GPS) locates a receiver by *<ConceptLink targetId="trilateracion">trilateration</ConceptLink>*: from the propagation times of the signals emitted by satellites with known positions, the receiver computes its <ConceptLink targetId="distancia">distance</ConceptLink> to each of them and obtains its coordinates by solving a system of equations. The central mathematical tool is the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-pitagoras" isDependency={true}>Pythagorean theorem</ConceptLink>, which in <ConceptLink targetId="geometria-analitica">analytic geometry</ConceptLink> gives the expression of the <ConceptLink targetId="metrica-euclidiana">Euclidean distance</ConceptLink> between two points.

<Separador />

### The Distance Equation

We first work in the <ConceptLink targetId="modelo-cartesiano">Cartesian plane</ConceptLink> $\mathbb{R}^2$. Let <VisualBind element="R" color="mora">$R = (x, y)$</VisualBind> be the unknown position of the receiver and <VisualBind element="s1" color="mora">$S_1 = (x_1, y_1)$</VisualBind> that of a satellite, with measured distance $d_1 = d(R, S_1)$. The coordinate differences are the <ConceptLink targetId="cateto">legs</ConceptLink> of a <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo-rectangulo">right triangle</ConceptLink> whose <ConceptLink targetId="hipotenusa">hypotenuse</ConceptLink> is the <ConceptLink targetId="segmento">segment</ConceptLink> $\overline{RS_1}$:

- <VisualBind element="catH1" color="mora">Horizontal leg</VisualBind>: $|x - x_1|$
- <VisualBind element="catV1" color="mora">Vertical leg</VisualBind>: $|y - y_1|$
- <VisualBind element="rad1" color="mora">Hypotenuse</VisualBind>: $d_1$

By the Pythagorean theorem, the receiver lies on the <ConceptLink targetId="circunferencia">circle</ConceptLink> with <ConceptLink targetId="centro">center</ConceptLink> $S_1$ and <ConceptLink targetId="radio">radius</ConceptLink> $d_1$:

<Formula>
  $$ (x - x_1)^2 + (y - y_1)^2 = d_1^2 $$
</Formula>

<Separador />

### Intersection of Circles

Each satellite adds an equation of the same kind:

- **One satellite ($S_1$)**: the receiver lies somewhere on the <VisualBind element="circ1" color="mora">circle</VisualBind> of radius <VisualBind element="rad1" color="mora">$d_1$</VisualBind>.
- **Two satellites ($S_1$ and $S_2$)**: the satellite <VisualBind element="s2" color="ocre">$S_2 = (x_2, y_2)$</VisualBind> contributes a <VisualBind element="circ2" color="ocre">second circle</VisualBind> of radius <VisualBind element="rad2" color="ocre">$d_2$</VisualBind>:
  <Formula>
    $$ (x - x_2)^2 + (y - y_2)^2 = d_2^2 $$
  </Formula>
  Subtracting the two equations cancels the terms $x^2$ and $y^2$ and leaves a linear equation: that of a <ConceptLink targetId="recta">line</ConceptLink> <ConceptLink targetId="perpendicular">perpendicular</ConceptLink> to $\overleftrightarrow{S_1S_2}$ (the <ConceptLink targetId="eje-radical">radical axis</ConceptLink>). Since a line meets a circle in at most two points, two circles with distinct centers meet in at most two points: the measurements from two satellites leave, in general, two possible positions.
- **Three satellites ($S_1$, $S_2$ and $S_3$)**: the satellite <VisualBind element="s3" color="pavo">$S_3 = (x_3, y_3)$</VisualBind> contributes the <VisualBind element="circ3" color="pavo">third circle</VisualBind> of radius <VisualBind element="rad3" color="pavo">$d_3$</VisualBind>:
  <Formula>
    $$ (x - x_3)^2 + (y - y_3)^2 = d_3^2 $$
  </Formula>
  Subtracting the first equation from the second and from the third gives two linear equations, whose lines are perpendicular to $\overleftrightarrow{S_1S_2}$ and to $\overleftrightarrow{S_1S_3}$. If the centers $S_1, S_2, S_3$ are not aligned (<ConceptLink targetId="colinealidad">not collinear</ConceptLink>), these lines are not <ConceptLink targetId="paralelas">parallel</ConceptLink> and meet in a single point: the position of the receiver <VisualBind element="R" color="mora">$R$</VisualBind> is determined. If the three centers are aligned, the ambiguity between two points symmetric with respect to the line of centers remains.

<Separador />

### Extension to Space and Clock Correction

In space $\mathbb{R}^3$, each measurement places the receiver on a <ConceptLink targetId="esfera">sphere</ConceptLink> with center $S_i = (x_i, y_i, z_i)$ and radius $d_i$, where $d_i^2 = (x - x_i)^2 + (y - y_i)^2 + (z - z_i)^2$. Moreover, the receiver's clock is not synchronized with the satellites' atomic clocks: the measured distances share a common error due to an unknown offset $\Delta t$. Therefore at least four satellites are needed, giving a <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-ecuaciones">system</ConceptLink> of four nonlinear equations in four unknowns $(x, y, z, \Delta t)$.
