<Capitular letra="G" />lobal Positioning System (GPS) technology is one of the most widespread engineering applications of analytic geometry and the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-pitagoras">Pythagorean theorem</ConceptLink>. This mathematical procedure, termed *trilateration*, calculates the absolute spatial coordinates of a terrestrial receiver by measuring the propagation times of electromagnetic signals transmitted from orbiting reference satellites.

<Separador />

### Metric Principle and the Distance Equation

The receiver <VisualBind element="R" color="mora">$R(x, y)$</VisualBind> determines its position in the <ConceptLink targetId="modelo-cartesiano">Cartesian plane</ConceptLink> by measuring the direct geometric distance to each broadcasting satellite $S_i$. For a satellite <VisualBind element="s1" color="mora">$S_1(x_1, y_1)$</VisualBind> with measured distance $d_1$, coordinate projections form the legs of a <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo-rectangulo">right-angled triangle</ConceptLink>:

- <VisualBind element="catH1" color="mora">Horizontal leg</VisualBind>: $\Delta x = |x - x_1|$
- <VisualBind element="catV1" color="mora">Vertical leg</VisualBind>: $\Delta y = |y - y_1|$
- <VisualBind element="rad1" color="mora">Hypotenuse (Euclidean distance)</VisualBind>: $d_1$

Applying the Pythagorean theorem establishes the quadratic equation of the signal <ConceptLink targetId="circunferencia">circle</ConceptLink> centered at $S_1$:

<Formula>
  $$ d_1^2 = (x - x_1)^2 + (y - y_1)^2 $$
</Formula>

<Separador />

### Intersection of Circles and Resolution

To uniquely resolve the unknown coordinates $(x, y)$ of the receiver, a simultaneous system of quadratic equations is solved:

- **One Satellite ($S_1$)**: Defines a <VisualBind element="circ1" color="mora">first circle</VisualBind> of radius <VisualBind element="rad1" color="mora">$d_1$</VisualBind> along which the receiver must lie.
- **Two Satellites ($S_1$ and $S_2$)**: Incorporating satellite <VisualBind element="s2" color="ocre">$S_2(x_2, y_2)$</VisualBind> introduces a <VisualBind element="circ2" color="ocre">second circle</VisualBind> of radius <VisualBind element="rad2" color="ocre">$d_2$</VisualBind>:
  <Formula>
    $$ d_2^2 = (x - x_2)^2 + (y - y_2)^2 $$
  </Formula>
  The intersection of both circles reduces candidate positions to exactly two points in the plane.
- **Three Satellites ($S_1$, $S_2$, and $S_3$)**: Incorporating satellite <VisualBind element="s3" color="pavo">$S_3(x_3, y_3)$</VisualBind> yields the <VisualBind element="circ3" color="pavo">third circle</VisualBind> of radius <VisualBind element="rad3" color="pavo">$d_3$</VisualBind>:
  <Formula>
    $$ d_3^2 = (x - x_3)^2 + (y - y_3)^2 $$
  </Formula>
  The simultaneous triple intersection eliminates all ambiguity, pinpointing the exact coordinates of receiver <VisualBind element="R" color="mora">$R(x, y)$</VisualBind>.

<Separador />

### Three-Dimensional Extension and Clock Bias Correction

In 3D physical space $\mathbb{R}^3$, each satellite defines a sphere $d_i = \sqrt{(x - x_i)^2 + (y - y_i)^2 + (z - z_i)^2}$. To correct for the clock bias $\Delta t$ in the receiver's internal quartz oscillator relative to satellite atomic clocks, a fourth satellite $S_4$ is required, resolving a non-linear $4 \times 4$ system for $(x, y, z, \Delta t)$.
