<Capitular letra="A" /> **bijection** (or bijective map) is a <ConceptLink targetId="funcion" isDependency={true}>function</ConceptLink> between two <ConceptLink targetId="conjunto" isDependency={true}>sets</ConceptLink> such that every element of the <ConceptLink targetId="codominio">codomain</ConceptLink> is the <ConceptLink targetId="imagen">image</ConceptLink> of exactly one element of the <ConceptLink targetId="dominio-funcion">domain</ConceptLink>.

A bijection thus pairs the elements of the two sets one to one, and the pairing can be traversed in the opposite direction: assigning to each element of the codomain the unique element of the domain of which it is the image defines the <ConceptLink targetId="funcion-inversa">inverse function</ConceptLink>.

<Definicion title="Formal definition of bijection">
  Let <VisualBind element="conjuntoA" color="carbon">$A$</VisualBind> and <VisualBind element="conjuntoB" color="carbon">$B$</VisualBind> be two <ConceptLink targetId="conjunto" isDependency={true}>sets</ConceptLink>. A <ConceptLink targetId="funcion" isDependency={true}>function</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="flechas" color="terracota">$f\colon A \longrightarrow B$</VisualBind> is a **bijection** if and only if it simultaneously satisfies two conditions:
  1. **<ConceptLink targetId="inyectividad" isDependency={true}>Injectivity</ConceptLink>:** Distinct elements of the domain have distinct images:
     $$
     \forall x_1, x_2 \in A, \quad f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2
     $$
  2. **<ConceptLink targetId="sobreyectividad" isDependency={true}>Surjectivity</ConceptLink>:** Every element of the codomain is the image of at least one element of the domain:
     $$
     \forall y \in B, \quad \exists x \in A \quad (f(x) = y)
     $$
  
  Equivalently, $f$ is bijective if and only if there exists an <ConceptLink targetId="funcion-inversa">inverse function</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="flechas-inversas" color="mora">$f^{-1}\colon B \longrightarrow A$</VisualBind> such that its <ConceptLink targetId="composicion-funciones">composition</ConceptLink> with $f$ gives the <ConceptLink targetId="funcion-identidad">identity</ConceptLink>: $f^{-1} \circ f = \operatorname{id}_A$ and $f \circ f^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_B$.
</Definicion>

In the figure, the “Reverse the arrows” button traverses each pair in the opposite direction: only when $f$ is bijective is the result a function $f^{-1}$ from $B$ to $A$.

Between <ConceptLink targetId="conjunto-finito">finite sets</ConceptLink> with the same number of elements, a function is injective if and only if it is surjective: in the figure, as long as all three arrows reach $B$, there is no way to break one condition without breaking the other.

### Uses of bijections

Three notions from different branches are formulated through bijections:
- **Size of a set:** Two sets have the same <ConceptLink targetId="cardinalidad">cardinality</ConceptLink> if and only if there exists a bijection between them ($\lvert A \rvert = \lvert B \rvert$).
- **Structural preservation:** An <ConceptLink targetId="isomorfismo">isomorphism</ConceptLink> is a bijection between <ConceptLink targetId="estructura-geometrica">structures</ConceptLink> that additionally preserves all relations and operations of their <ConceptLink targetId="signatura">signature</ConceptLink>.
- **Congruence of figures:** In geometry, the <ConceptLink targetId="congruencia-triangulos">congruence</ConceptLink> of two <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo">triangles</ConceptLink> (and, more generally, of two <ConceptLink targetId="poligono">polygons</ConceptLink>) is defined via a bijective correspondence between their <ConceptLink targetId="vertice">vertices</ConceptLink> that preserves the <ConceptLink targetId="congruencia">congruence</ConceptLink> of the homologous <ConceptLink targetId="lado">sides</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="angulo">angles</ConceptLink>.
