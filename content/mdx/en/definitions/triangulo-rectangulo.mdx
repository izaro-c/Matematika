<Capitular letra="A" /> **right triangle** is a <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo" isDependency={true}>triangle</ConceptLink> one of whose <ConceptLink targetId="angulo" isDependency={true}>angles</ConceptLink> is a <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-recto" isDependency={true}>right angle</ConceptLink>. The two <ConceptLink targetId="lado">sides</ConceptLink> forming that angle are called <VisualBind element="catetos" color="terracota"><ConceptLink targetId="cateto">legs</ConceptLink></VisualBind>, and the opposite side the <VisualBind element="segAB" color="pavo"><ConceptLink targetId="hipotenusa">hypotenuse</ConceptLink></VisualBind>.

The notion is stated without any <ConceptLink targetId="medida-angular">angle measure</ConceptLink> and belongs to <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-absoluto">absolute geometry</ConceptLink>. On it rest the congruence criteria specific to right triangles and, in Euclidean geometry, the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-pitagoras">Pythagorean theorem</ConceptLink>.

<Definicion title="Formal Definition of Right Triangle">
Let <VisualBind element="pA" color="carbon">$A$</VisualBind>, <VisualBind element="pB" color="carbon">$B$</VisualBind>, <VisualBind element="pC" color="carbon">$C$</VisualBind> $\in \mathcal{P}$ be three non-<ConceptLink targetId="colinealidad">collinear</ConceptLink> <ConceptLink targetId="punto" isDependency={true}>points</ConceptLink> of a <ConceptLink targetId="plano" isDependency={true}>plane</ConceptLink> $\pi \in \Pi$, determining the <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo" isDependency={true}>triangle</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="triangulo" color="musgo">$\triangle ABC$</VisualBind> $= \{\overline{AB}, \overline{BC}, \overline{CA}\}$.

The triangle $\triangle ABC$ is called a **right triangle** with right angle at the <ConceptLink targetId="vertice">vertex</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="pC" color="carbon">$C$</VisualBind> if the <ConceptLink targetId="recta-soporte">supporting lines</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="recta-CA" color="carbon">$\overleftrightarrow{CA}$</VisualBind> and <VisualBind element="recta-CB" color="carbon">$\overleftrightarrow{CB}$</VisualBind> are <ConceptLink targetId="perpendicular" isDependency={true}>perpendicular</ConceptLink>, that is, if <VisualBind element="angBCA" color="ocre">$\angle BCA$</VisualBind> is a right angle:

$$
\triangle ABC \text{ is right-angled at } C \iff \overleftrightarrow{CA} \perp \overleftrightarrow{CB}
$$

The <ConceptLink targetId="lado">side</ConceptLink> opposite the vertex $C$, <VisualBind element="segAB" color="pavo">$\overline{AB}$</VisualBind>, is called the <ConceptLink targetId="hipotenusa">hypotenuse</ConceptLink>, and the two sides meeting at $C$, <VisualBind element="segCA" color="terracota">$\overline{CA}$</VisualBind> and <VisualBind element="segCB" color="terracota">$\overline{CB}$</VisualBind>, are called the <VisualBind element="catetos" color="terracota"><ConceptLink targetId="cateto">legs</ConceptLink></VisualBind>.
</Definicion>

<SeccionPropiedades>
  <PropiedadesGrupo title="Angular Properties in Absolute Geometry">
    <PropiedadItem 
      id="unicidad-angulo-recto" 
      title="The Other Two Angles Are Acute"
      formula="\triangle ABC \text{ right-angled at } C \implies \angle CAB < \angle BCA \land \angle ABC < \angle BCA"
    >
      A triangle with a <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-recto">right angle</ConceptLink> cannot have another right angle or an <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-obtuso">obtuse angle</ConceptLink>: the angles at $A$ and $B$ are <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-agudo">acute</ConceptLink>. Indeed, the exterior angle at $C$ is supplementary to a right angle and hence also right; by the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-angulo-exterior">exterior angle theorem</ConceptLink>, each of the interior angles at $A$ and $B$ is <ConceptLink targetId="orden-angulos">smaller</ConceptLink> than it. The argument uses only the axioms of incidence, order, and congruence, and holds in <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-absoluto">absolute geometry</ConceptLink>.
    </PropiedadItem>

    <PropiedadItem 
      id="angulos-agudos-complementarios" 
      title="Complementarity of the Acute Angles"
      formula="|\angle CAB| + |\angle ABC| = 90^\circ"
    >
      In <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-euclidiano">Euclidean geometry</ConceptLink>, the two acute angles of a right triangle are <ConceptLink targetId="angulos-complementarios">complementary</ConceptLink>: the sum of their <ConceptLink targetId="medida-angular">measures</ConceptLink> is $90^\circ$. This follows from the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-suma-angulos-triangulo">triangle angle sum theorem</ConceptLink>, which depends on the <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-euclides">parallel axiom</ConceptLink>.
    </PropiedadItem>
  </PropiedadesGrupo>

  <PropiedadesGrupo title="Euclidean Metric Relations">
    <PropiedadItem 
      id="relacion-pitagorica" 
      title="Pythagorean Relation"
      formula="|AC|^2 + |BC|^2 = |AB|^2"
    >
      In every right triangle of the Euclidean plane, the sum of the squares of the <ConceptLink targetId="longitud">lengths</ConceptLink> of the legs equals the square of the length of the hypotenuse (<ConceptLink targetId="teorema-pitagoras">Pythagorean theorem</ConceptLink>).
    </PropiedadItem>
  </PropiedadesGrupo>
</SeccionPropiedades>

<Nota>
In <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-absoluto">absolute geometry</ConceptLink>, right triangles admit simplified <ConceptLink targetId="congruencia">congruence</ConceptLink> criteria: the leg-leg criterion, a special case of the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-congruencia-lal">SAS</ConceptLink> criterion, and the hypotenuse-leg criterion, both valid independently of the parallel axiom.
</Nota>
