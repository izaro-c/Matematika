<Capitular letra="A" /> **set** is a mathematical notion formalizing the collection into a unified whole of definite, distinct objects —termed elements—, related to the set through the <ConceptLink targetId="concepto-primitivo">primitive</ConceptLink> relation of membership.

It constitutes the universal language of modern mathematics. To prevent paradoxes in <ConceptLink targetId="logica">logic</ConceptLink> associated with unrestricted comprehension (such as Russell's paradox concerning the set of all sets that do not belong to themselves), axiomatic set theory (ZFC) restricts set formation through formal <ConceptLink targetId="axioma">axioms</ConceptLink> that ensure the deductive stability of operations.

<Definicion title="Membership, equality, and subsets">
  Let $x$ be an object and $A$ a set. The expression $x \in A$ formalizes that $x$ **belongs to** $A$ (is an element of $A$), while $x \notin A$ formalizes $\neg(x \in A)$.
  
  Two sets $A$ and $B$ are **equal** if and only if they possess exactly the same elements (Axiom of extensionality):
  $$
  A = B \iff \forall x \ (x \in A \iff x \in B)
  $$
  A set $A$ is a **subset** of $B$ (denoted $A \subseteq B$) if every element of $A$ belongs to $B$:
  $$
  A \subseteq B \iff \forall x \ (x \in A \implies x \in B)
  $$
</Definicion>

### Set operations and the geometrization of space

From given sets, new entities are constructed via elementary Boolean operations:
- The union $A \cup B = \{x \mid x \in A \lor x \in B\}$.
- The <ConceptLink targetId="interseccion">intersection</ConceptLink> $A \cap B = \{x \mid x \in A \land x \in B\}$. Two sets are <ConceptLink targetId="conjunto-disjunto">disjoint</ConceptLink> if $A \cap B = \emptyset$.
- The difference $A \setminus B = \{x \mid x \in A \land x \notin B\}$.
- The power set <ConceptLink targetId="conjunto-potencia">$\wp(A)$</ConceptLink>, formed by the totality of subsets of $A$.

In the formalization of the <ConceptLink targetId="geometria-sintetica">synthetic geometry</ConceptLink> of <ConceptLink targetId="hilbert">David Hilbert</ConceptLink>, <ConceptLink targetId="espacio">space</ConceptLink> is conceived as the set $\mathcal{P}$ of all <ConceptLink targetId="punto">points</ConceptLink>. Composite figures such as the <ConceptLink targetId="segmento">segment</ConceptLink>, the <ConceptLink targetId="semiplano">half-plane</ConceptLink>, and the <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo">triangle</ConceptLink> are specific subsets of $\mathcal{P}$, enabling the application of set algebra to the analysis of regions and boundaries.
