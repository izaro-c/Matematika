<Capitular letra="T" />he **derivative** of a <ConceptLink targetId="numero-real">real</ConceptLink> <ConceptLink targetId="funcion">function</ConceptLink> at a <ConceptLink targetId="punto">point</ConceptLink> is the <ConceptLink targetId="limite">limit</ConceptLink> of the <ConceptLink targetId="cociente-incremental">difference quotient</ConceptLink> as the increment tends to zero; it measures the instantaneous rate of change of the function. It is the central concept of <ConceptLink targetId="calculo-diferencial">differential calculus</ConceptLink>, introduced by <ConceptLink targetId="newton">Newton</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="leibniz">Leibniz</ConceptLink> and grounded on the notion of limit by <ConceptLink targetId="cauchy">Cauchy</ConceptLink>. Geometrically, $f'(x_0)$ is the <ConceptLink targetId="pendiente">slope</ConceptLink> of the <ConceptLink targetId="recta-tangente">tangent line</ConceptLink> to the <ConceptLink targetId="grafica-funcion">graph</ConceptLink> of $f$ at the point $(x_0, f(x_0))$.

<Definicion title="Derivative at a point">
  Let $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be an open <ConceptLink targetId="intervalo">interval</ConceptLink>, $f\colon I \longrightarrow \mathbb{R}$ a <ConceptLink targetId="funcion" isDependency={true}>function</ConceptLink> and $x_0 \in I$. The derivative of $f$ at $x_0$ is the <ConceptLink targetId="limite" isDependency={true}>limit</ConceptLink> of the <ConceptLink targetId="cociente-incremental" isDependency={true}>difference quotient</ConceptLink>
  $$
  f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h},
  $$
  whenever this limit exists in $\mathbb{R}$. In that case $f$ is said to be <ConceptLink targetId="funcion-derivable">differentiable</ConceptLink> at $x_0$.
</Definicion>

### Notations

Over the history of <ConceptLink targetId="analisis-matematico">mathematical analysis</ConceptLink>, several notations have become established for the derivative of $y = f(x)$:

| Notation | Expression | Main use |
|---|---|---|
| <ConceptLink targetId="lagrange">Lagrange</ConceptLink> | $f'(x), f''(x), \dots, f^{(n)}(x)$ | Real analysis |
| <ConceptLink targetId="leibniz">Leibniz</ConceptLink> | $\frac{df}{dx}, \frac{d^2f}{dx^2}, \dots, \frac{d^n f}{dx^n}$ | Calculus and <ConceptLink targetId="ecuacion-diferencial">differential equations</ConceptLink> |
| <ConceptLink targetId="newton">Newton</ConceptLink> | $\dot{y}, \ddot{y}$ | Mechanics: derivatives with respect to time |
| <ConceptLink targetId="euler">Euler</ConceptLink> | $D f, D^2 f, \dots, D^n f$ | <ConceptLink targetId="operador-diferencial">Differential operators</ConceptLink> |

<SeccionPropiedades>
  <PropiedadesGrupo title="Differentiability and continuity">
    <PropiedadItem id="derivabilidad-implica-continuidad" title="Differentiability implies continuity">
      If $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, then $f$ is <ConceptLink targetId="funcion-continua">continuous</ConceptLink> at $x_0$. The converse is false: $x \longmapsto \lvert x \rvert$ is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable at $0$.
    </PropiedadItem>
    <PropiedadItem id="linealidad-derivada" title="Linearity">
      If $f$ and $g$ are differentiable at $x_0$ and $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$, then $\alpha f + \beta g$ is differentiable at $x_0$ and $(\alpha f + \beta g)'(x_0) = \alpha f'(x_0) + \beta g'(x_0)$.
    </PropiedadItem>
  </PropiedadesGrupo>

  <PropiedadesGrupo title="Differentiation rules">
    <PropiedadItem id="regla-producto" title="Product rule (Leibniz rule)">
      If $f$ and $g$ are differentiable at $x_0$, then $(f g)'(x_0) = f'(x_0)\, g(x_0) + f(x_0)\, g'(x_0)$.
    </PropiedadItem>
    <PropiedadItem id="regla-cadena" title="Chain rule">
      If $g$ is differentiable at $x_0$ and $f$ is differentiable at $g(x_0)$, then the <ConceptLink targetId="composicion-funciones">composition</ConceptLink> $f \circ g$ is differentiable at $x_0$ and $(f \circ g)'(x_0) = f'(g(x_0))\, g'(x_0)$.
    </PropiedadItem>
  </PropiedadesGrupo>
</SeccionPropiedades>
