<Capitular letra="L" />ogical **consistency** (or syntactic consistency) is the property of a <ConceptLink targetId="teoria-formal">formal theory</ConceptLink> from which a formula and its negation cannot both be deduced.

In classical logic, an inconsistent theory proves every formula of its language by the <ConceptLink targetId="principio-explosion">principle of explosion</ConceptLink> (*ex falso quodlibet*). Its <ConceptLink targetId="teorema">theorems</ConceptLink> then no longer distinguish one formula from another.

<Definicion title="Syntactic and semantic consistency">
  Let $\mathcal{T}$ be a theory with axioms $\Phi$ formulated in a <ConceptLink targetId="lenguaje-formal">formal language</ConceptLink> $\mathcal{L}_\sigma$.
  
  $\mathcal{T}$ is said to be **syntactically consistent** if for no formula $\varphi$ is it possible to formally derive both $\varphi$ and its negation $\neg \varphi$:
  $$
  \mathcal{T} \text{ is consistent} \iff \forall \varphi \in \mathcal{L}_\sigma, \quad \neg (\Phi \vdash \varphi \land \Phi \vdash \neg \varphi)
  $$
  On the other hand, $\mathcal{T}$ is **semantically consistent** (or satisfiable) if there exists at least one <ConceptLink targetId="teoria-modelos">model</ConceptLink> $\mathcal{M}$ such that $\mathcal{M} \models \mathcal{T}$.
  
  By <ConceptLink targetId="godel">Gödel</ConceptLink>'s <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-completitud-godel">completeness theorem</ConceptLink> in first-order <ConceptLink targetId="logica">logic</ConceptLink>, both notions coincide:
  $$
  \Phi \nvdash \bot \iff \exists \mathcal{M} \ (\mathcal{M} \models \Phi)
  $$
</Definicion>

### Relative consistency in the foundations of geometry

By Gödel's <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-incompletitud-godel">second incompleteness theorem</ConceptLink>, no consistent, recursively axiomatizable formal theory that contains elementary arithmetic can prove its own consistency. Consistency proofs are, in general, relative (<ConceptLink targetId="consistencia-relativa">relative consistency</ConceptLink>): a model of one theory is built inside another, and the first is consistent if the second is.

- **<ConceptLink targetId="sistema-euclidiano">Euclidean geometry</ConceptLink>:** In Chapter II of the *Grundlagen*, <ConceptLink targetId="hilbert">David Hilbert</ConceptLink> established the relative consistency of Euclidean geometry by interpreting its <ConceptLink targetId="concepto-primitivo">primitive</ConceptLink> terms in the Cartesian <ConceptLink targetId="modelo-cartesiano">model</ConceptLink> $\mathbb{R}^3$. If the <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-cuerpo-real">theory of real numbers</ConceptLink> $\mathbb{R}$ is consistent, Euclidean geometry is consistent.
- **Non-Euclidean geometries:** Historically, neither <ConceptLink targetId="bolyai">Bolyai</ConceptLink> nor <ConceptLink targetId="lobachevski">Lobachevsky</ConceptLink> succeeded in proving the consistency of <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-hiperbolico">hyperbolic geometry</ConceptLink>. It was <ConceptLink targetId="beltrami">Eugenio Beltrami</ConceptLink> (1868), Felix <ConceptLink targetId="klein">Klein</ConceptLink>, and <ConceptLink targetId="poincare">Henri Poincaré</ConceptLink> who proved it using analytic models in the Euclidean <ConceptLink targetId="plano">plane</ConceptLink>, such as the Poincaré <ConceptLink targetId="modelo-poincare">disk</ConceptLink>; hyperbolic consistency thus reduces to Euclidean consistency.
