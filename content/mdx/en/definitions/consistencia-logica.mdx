<Capitular letra="L" />**ogical consistency** (or syntactic consistency) is the property of a <ConceptLink targetId="teoria-formal">formal theory</ConceptLink> from which a <ConceptLink targetId="formula">formula</ConceptLink> and its <ConceptLink targetId="negacion">negation</ConceptLink> cannot both be deduced.

In classical <ConceptLink targetId="logica">logic</ConceptLink>, an inconsistent theory proves every formula of its language by the <ConceptLink targetId="principio-explosion">principle of explosion</ConceptLink> (*ex falso quodlibet*). Its <ConceptLink targetId="teorema">theorems</ConceptLink> then no longer distinguish one formula from another.

<Definicion title="Syntactic and semantic consistency">
  Let $\mathcal{T}$ be a theory with <ConceptLink targetId="axioma">axioms</ConceptLink> $\Phi$ formulated in a <ConceptLink targetId="lenguaje-formal">formal language</ConceptLink> $\mathcal{L}_\sigma$.
  
  $\mathcal{T}$ is said to be **syntactically consistent** if for no <ConceptLink targetId="formula">formula</ConceptLink> $\varphi$ is it possible to formally derive both $\varphi$ and its <ConceptLink targetId="negacion">negation</ConceptLink> $\neg \varphi$ (equivalently, $\Phi \nvdash \bot$, where $\bot$ is a contradiction):
  $$
  \mathcal{T} \text{ is consistent} \iff \forall \varphi \in \mathcal{L}_\sigma, \quad \neg (\Phi \vdash \varphi \land \Phi \vdash \neg \varphi)
  $$
  On the other hand, $\mathcal{T}$ is **semantically consistent** (or satisfiable) if there exists a <ConceptLink targetId="estructura-geometrica">structure</ConceptLink> $\mathcal{M}$ such that $\mathcal{M} \models \Phi$, that is, a <ConceptLink targetId="modelo">model</ConceptLink> of $\mathcal{T}$.
  
  By the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-correccion">soundness theorem</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="godel">Gödel</ConceptLink>'s <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-completitud-godel">completeness theorem</ConceptLink> in first-order <ConceptLink targetId="logica">logic</ConceptLink>, both notions coincide:
  $$
  \Phi \nvdash \bot \iff \exists \mathcal{M} \ (\mathcal{M} \models \Phi)
  $$
</Definicion>

### Relative consistency in the foundations of geometry

By Gödel's <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-incompletitud-godel">second incompleteness theorem</ConceptLink>, no consistent, recursively axiomatizable formal theory that contains elementary arithmetic can prove its own consistency. Consistency proofs are, in general, relative (<ConceptLink targetId="consistencia-relativa">relative consistency</ConceptLink>): a model of one theory is built inside another, and the first is consistent if the second is.

- **<ConceptLink targetId="sistema-euclidiano">Euclidean geometry</ConceptLink>:** In Chapter II of the *Grundlagen*, <ConceptLink targetId="hilbert">David Hilbert</ConceptLink> interpreted the <ConceptLink targetId="concepto-primitivo">primitive</ConceptLink> terms of Euclidean geometry in a <ConceptLink targetId="modelo-cartesiano">Cartesian model</ConceptLink> whose coordinates belong to a countable <ConceptLink targetId="cuerpo">field</ConceptLink> $\Omega$ of <ConceptLink targetId="numero-real">real</ConceptLink> algebraic numbers. He works it out in the plane, and the case of <ConceptLink targetId="espacio">space</ConceptLink> is analogous. This proves the relative consistency of axioms I–IV and V.1; to include the <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-completitud">completeness axiom</ConceptLink> (V.2), coordinates are taken in $\mathbb{R}$. Any contradiction in the axioms would carry over to the arithmetic of the coordinate field.
- **Non-Euclidean geometries:** Historically, neither <ConceptLink targetId="bolyai">Bolyai</ConceptLink> nor <ConceptLink targetId="lobachevski">Lobachevsky</ConceptLink> succeeded in proving the consistency of <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-hiperbolico">hyperbolic geometry</ConceptLink>. It was <ConceptLink targetId="beltrami">Eugenio Beltrami</ConceptLink> (1868, using surfaces of constant <ConceptLink targetId="curvatura">curvature</ConceptLink> and a disk model), <ConceptLink targetId="klein">Felix Klein</ConceptLink> (1871), and <ConceptLink targetId="poincare">Henri Poincaré</ConceptLink> (1882) who proved it using Euclidean models, including ones in the <ConceptLink targetId="plano">plane</ConceptLink>, such as the Poincaré <ConceptLink targetId="modelo-poincare">disk</ConceptLink>; hyperbolic consistency thus reduces to Euclidean consistency.
