<Capitular letra="A" /> plane is a continuous two-dimensional geometric entity that extends indefinitely in all directions of its domain without intrinsic curvature. In synthetic axiomatic frameworks, it is a fundamental primitive notion serving as the canonical space for planar geometry and trigonometry.

<Definicion title="">
  Given three non-collinear points <VisualBind element="pA" color="musgo">$A$</VisualBind>, <VisualBind element="pB" color="musgo">$B$</VisualBind>, and <VisualBind element="pC" color="musgo">$C$</VisualBind> in an affine or Euclidean space $\mathcal{E}$, the plane <VisualBind element="planeMesh" color="musgo">$\mathcal{P}$</VisualBind> determined by them is the two-dimensional set generated by all their affine combinations:
  $$
  \mathcal{P} = \{ P \in \mathcal{E} \mid P = \alpha A + \beta B + \gamma C, \, \alpha + \beta + \gamma = 1, \, \alpha, \beta, \gamma \in \mathbb{R} \}
  $$
</Definicion>

### Synthetic geometry

In Hilbert's formal system, planes constitute the third class of primitive objects governed by the axioms of spatial incidence:
- **Determination by three points**: Three non-collinear points determine a unique plane.
- **Line inclusion**: If two points of a <ConceptLink targetId="recta">line</ConceptLink> belong to a plane, the entire line lies within that plane.
- **Intersection of planes**: If two distinct planes share a common point, their intersection is exactly a <ConceptLink targetId="recta">line</ConceptLink>.
- **Plane separation**: Any line contained in a plane partitions the remaining points into two disjoint convex half-planes.

### Analytic geometry

In the three-dimensional Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^3$, a plane is described algebraically by:
- **General implicit equation**: $Ax + By + Cz + D = 0$, with $(A, B, C) \neq (0, 0, 0)$, where $\vec{n} = (A, B, C)$ is the normal vector orthogonal to the plane.
- **Vector parametric form**: $\vec{r}(u, v) = \vec{p}_0 + u\vec{v}_1 + v\vec{v}_2$, where $\vec{v}_1 \nparallel \vec{v}_2$ and $u, v \in \mathbb{R}$.

### Topology and differential geometry

Topologically, the Euclidean plane endowed with its standard metric topology is homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^2$. As a 2-dimensional smooth Riemannian manifold, its Gaussian curvature vanishes identically ($K = 0$).
