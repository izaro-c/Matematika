<Capitular letra="I" />**ntersection** is an <ConceptLink targetId="operacion-binaria">operation</ConceptLink> on <ConceptLink targetId="conjunto" isDependency={true}>sets</ConceptLink> that gathers the elements belonging to each of them at once. It corresponds to the <ConceptLink targetId="conjuncion">conjunction</ConceptLink> connective ($\land$) of propositional <ConceptLink targetId="logica">logic</ConceptLink>: an element is in the intersection of two sets if it is in the first and it is in the second.

It makes it possible to define composite geometric regions, such as the interior of an angle, and to describe the points common to two figures.

<Definicion title="Formal definition of intersection">
  Let <VisualBind element="conjuntoA" color="terracota">$A$</VisualBind> and <VisualBind element="conjuntoB" color="pavo">$B$</VisualBind> be two sets. The **intersection** of $A$ and $B$, denoted <VisualBind element="interseccionAB" color="mora">$A \cap B$</VisualBind>, is the set of the elements of $A$ that are also in $B$:
  $$
  A \cap B = \{x \in A \mid x \in B\}
  $$
  The <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-separacion" isDependency={true}>axiom of separation</ConceptLink> guarantees that $A \cap B$ is a set, and for every $x$ we have $x \in A \cap B \iff (x \in A \land x \in B)$.

  For a non-empty family of sets $\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ and any index $i_0 \in I$, its intersection is:
  $$
  \bigcap_{i \in I} A_i = \{x \in A_{i_0} \mid \forall i \in I, \ x \in A_i\}
  $$
  The result does not depend on the chosen index $i_0$. The family must be non-empty: if $I = \emptyset$, every object would satisfy the condition, and the collection of all objects is not a set.
</Definicion>

### Algebraic properties

Intersection satisfies <ConceptLink targetId="conmutatividad">commutativity</ConceptLink> ($A \cap B = B \cap A$), <ConceptLink targetId="asociatividad">associativity</ConceptLink> ($A \cap (B \cap C) = (A \cap B) \cap C$) and <ConceptLink targetId="idempotencia">idempotence</ConceptLink> ($A \cap A = A$). The set $A \cap B$ is a <ConceptLink targetId="subconjunto">subset</ConceptLink> of both $A$ and $B$, and $A \cap B = A$ if and only if $A \subseteq B$. Moreover, intersection is <ConceptLink targetId="distributividad">distributive</ConceptLink> over <ConceptLink targetId="union">union</ConceptLink>: $A \cap (B \cup C) = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C)$. When $A \cap B$ is the <ConceptLink targetId="conjunto-vacio">empty set</ConceptLink>, $A$ and $B$ are <ConceptLink targetId="conjunto-disjunto">disjoint sets</ConceptLink>.

### Intersections in geometry

By <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-incidencia-1">Axiom I.1</ConceptLink>, two distinct <ConceptLink targetId="recta">lines</ConceptLink> have at most one common <ConceptLink targetId="punto">point</ConceptLink>. Hence the intersection of their <ConceptLink targetId="traza">traces</ConceptLink> tells apart the two positions of two distinct <ConceptLink targetId="coplanaridad">coplanar</ConceptLink> lines. Two <ConceptLink targetId="secante">secant lines</ConceptLink> $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ have exactly one common point, $\operatorname{tr}(\ell_1) \cap \operatorname{tr}(\ell_2) = \{P\}$, and two distinct <ConceptLink targetId="paralelas">parallel lines</ConceptLink> have none: $\operatorname{tr}(\ell_1) \cap \operatorname{tr}(\ell_2) = \emptyset$.

The intersection of a non-empty family of <ConceptLink targetId="conjunto-convexo">convex sets</ConceptLink> is convex: if two points $X$ and $Y$ lie in all of them, the <ConceptLink targetId="segmento">segment</ConceptLink> $\overline{XY}$ is contained in each one and therefore in the intersection. Open <ConceptLink targetId="semiplano">half-planes</ConceptLink> are convex by the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-separacion-plano">plane separation theorem</ConceptLink>, so the regions obtained by intersecting them are convex too:
- The <ConceptLink targetId="interior-angulo">interior</ConceptLink> of an <ConceptLink targetId="angulo">angle</ConceptLink> is the intersection of two open half-planes: for each side, the one bounded by its <ConceptLink targetId="recta-soporte">supporting line</ConceptLink> that contains the other side. If $Q$ and $R$ are points of $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ other than $P$, then $\operatorname{Int}(\angle QPR) = \mathcal{H}_{\ell_1}(R) \cap \mathcal{H}_{\ell_2}(Q)$.
- The <ConceptLink targetId="interior-triangulo">interior</ConceptLink> of a <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo">triangle</ConceptLink> is the intersection of three open half-planes: for each <ConceptLink targetId="lado">side</ConceptLink>, the one bounded by its supporting line that contains the opposite <ConceptLink targetId="vertice">vertex</ConceptLink>. The interior of a convex <ConceptLink targetId="poligono">polygon</ConceptLink> is obtained in the same way, with one half-plane per side.
