<Capitular letra="A" /> parallelogram is a <ConceptLink targetId="cuadrilatero" isDependency={true}>quadrilateral</ConceptLink> whose two pairs of opposite <ConceptLink targetId="lado">sides</ConceptLink> lie on <ConceptLink targetId="paralelas" isDependency={true}>parallel lines</ConceptLink>.

Parallelism is required of the supporting <ConceptLink targetId="recta" isDependency={true}>lines</ConceptLink> of the sides, not of the <ConceptLink targetId="segmento">segments</ConceptLink>. The definition makes sense in <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-absoluto">absolute geometry</ConceptLink>, but the metric properties that characterise the parallelogram (congruent opposite sides, diagonals that bisect each other) depend on the <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-euclides">parallel axiom</ConceptLink>.

<Definicion title="Definition of parallelogram">
A <ConceptLink targetId="cuadrilatero">quadrilateral</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="poligono" color="carbon">$ABCD$</VisualBind> of a plane $\sigma$ is a **parallelogram** if the supporting lines of its <VisualBind element="lados-opuestos" color="carbon">opposite sides</VisualBind> are <ConceptLink targetId="paralelas">parallel</ConceptLink>:

$$
ABCD \text{ is a parallelogram} \iff \overleftrightarrow{AB} \parallel \overleftrightarrow{CD} \ \land \ \overleftrightarrow{BC} \parallel \overleftrightarrow{DA}.
$$

Since opposite sides of a quadrilateral are disjoint, the two lines of each pair are distinct and their <ConceptLink targetId="traza">traces</ConceptLink> do not meet.
</Definicion>

### Special cases

| Figure | Additional condition |
|---|---|
| <ConceptLink targetId="rombo">Rhombus</ConceptLink> | Four <VisualBind element="lados-iguales" color="pavo">congruent sides</VisualBind> |
| <ConceptLink targetId="rectangulo">Rectangle</ConceptLink> | Four <VisualBind element="angulos-rectos" color="ocre">right angles</VisualBind> (<ConceptLink targetId="angulo-recto">right angle</ConceptLink>) |
| <ConceptLink targetId="cuadrado">Square</ConceptLink> | Both a rhombus and a rectangle |

Every quadrilateral with four congruent sides is a parallelogram, even without the parallel axiom: by the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-congruencia-lll">SSS criterion</ConceptLink> the diagonal $\overline{AC}$ gives $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle CDA$, and congruence of the alternate interior angles implies parallelism (<ConceptLink targetId="teorema-angulos-alternos-internos">alternate interior angles theorem</ConceptLink>).

      formula="\overline{AB} \cong \overline{CD}, \quad \overline{BC} \cong \overline{DA}, \quad \angle DAB \cong \angle BCD, \quad \angle ABC \cong \angle CDA"

      In the <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-euclidiano">Euclidean system</ConceptLink>, the opposite sides of a parallelogram are <ConceptLink targetId="congruencia">congruent</ConceptLink>, and so are its <VisualBind element="angulos-opuestos" color="ocre">opposite angles</VisualBind> (Euclid I.34). The diagonal $\overline{AC}$ makes congruent alternate interior angles with the parallels, which requires the <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-euclides">parallel axiom</ConceptLink>, and the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-congruencia-ala">ASA criterion</ConceptLink> gives $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle CDA$.
    </PropiedadItem>

    <PropiedadItem
      id="diagonales-paralelogramo"

    <PropiedadItem

      title="Area"
      formula="\operatorname{A}(ABCD) = b\,h"

      The <ConceptLink targetId="teoria-medida-area">area</ConceptLink> of a parallelogram is the product of the <ConceptLink targetId="longitud">length</ConceptLink> $b$ of a side and the corresponding <ConceptLink targetId="altura">height</ConceptLink> $h$: it is equal by complements to a <ConceptLink targetId="rectangulo">rectangle</ConceptLink> with the same base and height, whose area is given by the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-area-rectangulo">rectangle area theorem</ConceptLink>.
    </PropiedadItem>
