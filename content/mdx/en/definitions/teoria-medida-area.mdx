<Capitular letra="A" />n area <ConceptLink targetId="funcion">function</ConceptLink> on a <ConceptLink targetId="plano" isDependency={true}>plane</ConceptLink> $\sigma$ assigns to each <ConceptLink targetId="region-poligonal" isDependency={true}>polygonal region</ConceptLink> of $\sigma$ a positive <ConceptLink targetId="numero-real">real number</ConceptLink>, in such a way that <ConceptLink targetId="congruencia" isDependency={true}>congruent</ConceptLink> triangular regions have the same area, the area of the union of two regions with disjoint interiors is the sum of their areas, and the <ConceptLink targetId="cuadrado" isDependency={true}>square</ConceptLink> of unit side has area $1$.

The four conditions turn <ConceptLink targetId="euclides">Euclid</ConceptLink>'s common notions ("things which coincide with one another are equal", "the whole is greater than the part") into numbers, and they suffice to derive the classical formulas: they yield <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-area-aditividad">finite additivity</ConceptLink>, the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-area-rectangulo">area of the rectangle</ConceptLink> and the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-area-triangulo">area of the triangle</ConceptLink>, on which the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-tales">intercept theorem</ConceptLink> and the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-pitagoras">Pythagorean theorem</ConceptLink> rest. That a function with these properties exists, and that it is unique, is not part of the definition: it is a <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-existencia-area">theorem</ConceptLink> proved by <ConceptLink targetId="hilbert">Hilbert</ConceptLink> in chapter IV of the *Grundlagen der Geometrie*.

<Definicion title="Area function on polygonal regions">
A <ConceptLink targetId="region-triangular" isDependency={true}>triangular region</ConceptLink> is the union of the trace of a <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo" isDependency={true}>triangle</ConceptLink> and its <ConceptLink targetId="interior-triangulo" isDependency={true}>interior</ConceptLink>, $\operatorname{tr}(\triangle ABC) \cup \operatorname{Int}(\triangle ABC)$; a <ConceptLink targetId="region-poligonal" isDependency={true}>polygonal region</ConceptLink> is a finite union of triangular regions contained in $\operatorname{tr}(\sigma)$. Let $\mathcal{R}_\sigma$ be the <ConceptLink targetId="conjunto">set</ConceptLink> of polygonal regions of $\sigma$. Two regions $R_1, R_2 \in \mathcal{R}_\sigma$ have **disjoint interiors** if their intersection is contained in a finite union of <ConceptLink targetId="segmento" isDependency={true}>segments</ConceptLink>. We write $\operatorname{A}(\triangle ABC)$ for the area of the triangular region determined by $\triangle ABC$.

An **area function** is a map

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{A}\colon \mathcal{R}_\sigma &\longrightarrow \mathbb{R}_{>0} \\
R &\longmapsto \operatorname{A}(R)
\end{aligned}
$$

satisfying:
1. **Positivity**: $\operatorname{A}(R) > 0$ for every $R \in \mathcal{R}_\sigma$.
2. **Invariance under congruence**: if $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle A'B'C'$ (<ConceptLink targetId="congruencia-triangulos" isDependency={true}>congruent triangles</ConceptLink>), then $\operatorname{A}(\triangle ABC) = \operatorname{A}(\triangle A'B'C')$.
3. **Additivity**: if $R = R_1 \cup R_2$ with $R_1$ and $R_2$ having disjoint interiors, then $\operatorname{A}(R) = \operatorname{A}(R_1) + \operatorname{A}(R_2)$.
4. **Normalisation**: if $Q$ is the region of a square whose <ConceptLink targetId="lado">side</ConceptLink> is congruent to the <ConceptLink targetId="segmento-unidad" isDependency={true}>unit segment</ConceptLink>, then $\operatorname{A}(Q) = 1$.
</Definicion>

### Equidecomposability and equicomplementability

Two polygonal regions $P, Q \in \mathcal{R}_\sigma$ are **<ConceptLink targetId="equidescomponibilidad">equidecomposable</ConceptLink>** if they admit decompositions $P = T_1 \cup \dots \cup T_n$ and $Q = T'_1 \cup \dots \cup T'_n$ into triangular regions with pairwise disjoint interiors such that $T_i \cong T'_i$ for each $i$. They are **equicomplementable** if there exist equidecomposable regions $P'$ and $Q'$, with interiors disjoint from those of $P$ and $Q$ respectively, such that $P \cup P'$ and $Q \cup Q'$ are equidecomposable.

By invariance and additivity, two equidecomposable or equicomplementable regions have the same area. In chapter IV Hilbert constructs a measure of content without using the <ConceptLink targetId="continuidad">axioms of continuity</ConceptLink> and proves that two regions are equicomplementable if and only if they have the same measure; with the <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-arquimedes">Archimedean axiom</ConceptLink>, equicomplementability and equidecomposability coincide (<ConceptLink targetId="teorema-bolyai-gerwien">Bolyai–Gerwien theorem</ConceptLink>).

    </PropiedadItem>

      Two <ConceptLink targetId="equidescomponibilidad">equidecomposable</ConceptLink> regions have the same area, since their pieces are pairwise <ConceptLink targetId="congruencia-triangulos">congruent triangles</ConceptLink>.
    </PropiedadItem>
