<Capitular letra="T" />wo <ConceptLink targetId="coplanaridad">coplanar</ConceptLink> <ConceptLink targetId="recta" isDependency={true}>lines</ConceptLink> are **secant** (or intersecting) if their intersection consists of exactly a single <ConceptLink targetId="punto" isDependency={true}>point</ConceptLink>. Similarly, a line is a **transversal secant** with respect to a family of coplanar lines if it intersects each of them at distinct points.

By <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-incidencia-1">Axiom I.1</ConceptLink>, two distinct lines cannot share more than one point without coinciding entirely. In the same <ConceptLink targetId="plano" isDependency={true}>plane</ConceptLink>, two lines are either <ConceptLink targetId="paralelas">parallel</ConceptLink>—in which case they coincide or share no points—or secant. Transversal secant lines serve as the essential geometric tool to establish fundamental angular relationships, such as in the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-angulos-alternos-internos">alternate interior angles theorem</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-tales">Thales's theorem</ConceptLink>.

<Definicion title="Formal definition of secant lines and transversal line">
Let <VisualBind element="lineL1" color="terracota">$\ell_1$</VisualBind>, <VisualBind element="lineL2" color="carbon">$\ell_2$</VisualBind> $\in \mathcal{L}$ be two distinct coplanar <ConceptLink targetId="recta" isDependency={true}>lines</ConceptLink> in a <ConceptLink targetId="plano" isDependency={true}>plane</ConceptLink> $\pi \in \Pi$, that is, $\operatorname{tr}(\ell_1) \cup \operatorname{tr}(\ell_2) \subseteq \operatorname{tr}(\pi)$. The lines $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ are **secant** if they simultaneously <ConceptLink targetId="incidencia" isDependency={true}>incident</ConceptLink> with a unique <ConceptLink targetId="punto" isDependency={true}>point</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="pP" color="ocre">$P$</VisualBind> $\in \mathcal{P}$:

$$
\operatorname{sec}(\ell_1, \ell_2) \iff (\ell_1 \neq \ell_2) \land \exists! P \in \mathcal{P} \, (P \,\mathbf{I}\, \ell_1 \land P \,\mathbf{I}\, \ell_2)
$$

Equivalently, in terms of their <ConceptLink targetId="traza">point trace</ConceptLink>, $\operatorname{tr}(\ell_1) \cap \operatorname{tr}(\ell_2) = \{P\}$. The point $P$ is called the **point of intersection** of both lines.

Likewise, a line <VisualBind element="lineT" color="musgo">$t$</VisualBind> $\in \mathcal{L}$ contained in the same plane $\pi$ ($\operatorname{tr}(t) \subseteq \operatorname{tr}(\pi)$) is a **transversal secant** to $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ if it is secant to each of them at distinct points <VisualBind element="pP1" color="pavo">$P_1$</VisualBind> $\neq$ <VisualBind element="pP2" color="pavo">$P_2$</VisualBind>:

$$
\operatorname{transv}(t; \ell_1, \ell_2) \iff \exists P_1, P_2 \in \mathcal{P} \, (P_1 \neq P_2 \land P_1 \,\mathbf{I}\, t \land P_1 \,\mathbf{I}\, \ell_1 \land P_2 \,\mathbf{I}\, t \land P_2 \,\mathbf{I}\, \ell_2)
$$
</Definicion>
