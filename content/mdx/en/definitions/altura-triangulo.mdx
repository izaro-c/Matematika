<Capitular letra="T" />he **altitude** of a <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo" isDependency={true}>triangle</ConceptLink> corresponding to a given <ConceptLink targetId="vertice">vertex</ConceptLink> is the <ConceptLink targetId="segmento">segment</ConceptLink> drawn from that vertex <ConceptLink targetId="perpendicular" isDependency={true}>perpendicularly</ConceptLink> to the <ConceptLink targetId="recta" isDependency={true}>carrier line</ConceptLink> of the opposite <ConceptLink targetId="lado">side</ConceptLink>, with endpoint at the foot of that perpendicular.

By the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-existencia-perpendicular">existence and uniqueness theorem of the perpendicular</ConceptLink>, from each vertex there is a unique perpendicular line to the opposite carrier line, determining exactly three <VisualBind element="alturas" color="ocre">altitudes</VisualBind> in every triangle. In metric Euclidean geometry, the term also designates the <ConceptLink targetId="longitud">length</ConceptLink> of this segment, a fundamental parameter for calculating the <ConceptLink targetId="teoria-medida-area">area</ConceptLink> of the <ConceptLink targetId="region-triangular">triangular region</ConceptLink>.

<Definicion title="Formal definition of the altitude of a triangle">
Let $\triangle ABC = \{\overline{AB}, \overline{BC}, \overline{CA}\}$ be a <VisualBind element="polygonABC" color="musgo"><ConceptLink targetId="triangulo" isDependency={true}>triangle</ConceptLink></VisualBind> in a <ConceptLink targetId="plano">plane</ConceptLink> $\pi \in \Pi$, determined by three non-collinear <ConceptLink targetId="punto">points</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="pA" color="terracota">$A$</VisualBind>, <VisualBind element="pB" color="terracota">$B$</VisualBind>, <VisualBind element="pC" color="terracota">$C$</VisualBind> $\in \mathcal{P}$.

For vertex <VisualBind element="pC" color="terracota">$C$</VisualBind> and the opposite <ConceptLink targetId="recta" isDependency={true}>carrier line</ConceptLink> $\overleftrightarrow{AB}$, the unique perpendicular line to $\overleftrightarrow{AB}$ passing through $C$ intersects $\operatorname{tr}(\overleftrightarrow{AB})$ at a unique point <VisualBind element="footCAB" color="ocre">$H_c \in \mathcal{P}$</VisualBind>, called the foot of the perpendicular:

$$
\overleftrightarrow{CH_c} \perp \overleftrightarrow{AB}, \quad H_c \in \operatorname{tr}(\overleftrightarrow{AB})
$$

The **altitude** corresponding to vertex $C$ is the closed <ConceptLink targetId="segmento">segment</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="segAlturaCAB" color="ocre">$\overline{CH_c}$</VisualBind>:

$$
h_c = \overline{CH_c}
$$

Analogously, vertices <VisualBind element="pA" color="terracota">$A$</VisualBind> and <VisualBind element="pB" color="terracota">$B$</VisualBind> determine the feet <VisualBind element="footABC" color="ocre">$H_a$</VisualBind> and <VisualBind element="footBAC" color="ocre">$H_b$</VisualBind> on the carrier lines $\overleftrightarrow{BC}$ and $\overleftrightarrow{CA}$, defining altitudes <VisualBind element="segAlturaABC" color="ocre">$\overline{AH_a}$</VisualBind> and <VisualBind element="segAlturaBAC" color="ocre">$\overline{BH_b}$</VisualBind>.
</Definicion>
