<Capitular letra="A" /> triangle is the planar geometric figure constituted by three non-<ConceptLink targetId="colinealidad" isDependency={true}>collinear</ConceptLink> <ConceptLink targetId="punto">points</ConceptLink> and the three <ConceptLink targetId="segmento" isDependency={true}>segments</ConceptLink> joining them pairwise.

The triangle is neither a metric magnitude nor an area measure, but a formal structure constructed from the <ConceptLink targetId="estar-entre">betweenness</ConceptLink> relation and non-<ConceptLink targetId="colinealidad">collinearity</ConceptLink> in the <ConceptLink targetId="plano">plane</ConceptLink>.

<Definicion title="Formal Definition of Triangle">
Given three <ConceptLink targetId="punto">points</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="vertice-a" color="musgo">$A$</VisualBind>, <VisualBind element="vertice-b" color="musgo">$B$</VisualBind>, and <VisualBind element="vertice-c" color="musgo">$C$</VisualBind> in the primitive <ConceptLink targetId="dominio">domain</ConceptLink> $\mathcal{P}$ such that the triple $\{A, B, C\}$ is not <ConceptLink targetId="colinealidad">collinear</ConceptLink>, the **triangle** <VisualBind element="triangulo" color="musgo">$\triangle ABC$</VisualBind> is the <ConceptLink targetId="conjunto">set</ConceptLink> constituted by the three <ConceptLink targetId="segmento">segments</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="lado-ab" color="pavo">$\overline{AB}$</VisualBind>, <VisualBind element="lado-bc" color="terracota">$\overline{BC}$</VisualBind>, and <VisualBind element="lado-ca" color="ocre">$\overline{CA}$</VisualBind>:

$$
\triangle ABC = \{ \overline{AB}, \overline{BC}, \overline{CA} \}
$$

The segments $\overline{AB}$, $\overline{BC}$, and $\overline{CA}$ are called the <ConceptLink targetId="lado">sides</ConceptLink> of the triangle, and their endpoints $A$, $B$, and $C$ are called the <ConceptLink targetId="vertice">vertices</ConceptLink>. Its associated <ConceptLink targetId="traza">**point trace**</ConceptLink> $\operatorname{tr}(\triangle ABC) \subset \mathcal{P}$ is the set union of the <ConceptLink targetId="traza">traces</ConceptLink> of its sides:

$$
\operatorname{tr}(\triangle ABC) = \operatorname{tr}(\overline{AB}) \cup \operatorname{tr}(\overline{BC}) \cup \operatorname{tr}(\overline{CA})
$$
</Definicion>

  <PropiedadesGrupo title="Structural and Order Properties">
    <PropiedadItem 
      id="simetria-vertices" 
      title="Invariance under Vertex Permutations"
      formula="\triangle ABC = \triangle \sigma(A)\sigma(B)\sigma(C) \quad (\forall \sigma \in S_3)"
    >
      Since it is defined from the unordered set of its sides ($\triangle ABC = \{ \overline{AB}, \overline{BC}, \overline{CA} \}$), the triangle is strictly invariant with respect to the order in which its vertices are stated: $\triangle ABC = \triangle BCA = \triangle CAB = \triangle BAC = \triangle ACB = \triangle CBA$, satisfying <ConceptLink targetId="igualdad-matematica">set equality</ConceptLink>.
    </PropiedadItem>

    <PropiedadItem 
      id="coplanaridad-soporte" 
      title="Coplanarity and Unique Supporting Plane"
      formula="\exists! \pi \in \Pi : \operatorname{tr}(\triangle ABC) \subset \operatorname{tr}(\pi)"
    >
      By Axioms <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-incidencia-4">I.4</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-incidencia-5">I.5</ConceptLink>, the non-<ConceptLink targetId="colinealidad">collinearity</ConceptLink> of the vertex set $\{A, B, C\}$ uniquely determines a single <ConceptLink targetId="plano">plane</ConceptLink> $\pi \in \Pi$ that contains the entire <ConceptLink targetId="traza">point trace</ConceptLink> of the triangle: $\operatorname{tr}(\triangle ABC) \subseteq \operatorname{tr}(\pi)$.
    </PropiedadItem>

    <PropiedadItem 
      id="interior-triangular" 
      title="Triangular Interior as a Convex Region"
      formula="\operatorname{Int}(\triangle ABC) = \mathcal{H}_{\overleftrightarrow{AB}}(C) \cap \mathcal{H}_{\overleftrightarrow{BC}}(A) \cap \mathcal{H}_{\overleftrightarrow{CA}}(B)"
    >
      Every triangle $\triangle ABC$ in a plane $\pi$ defines an open and bounded region known as the **interior of the triangle** ($\operatorname{Int}(\triangle ABC)$), formally defined as the <ConceptLink targetId="interseccion">intersection</ConceptLink> of the three open <ConceptLink targetId="semiplano">half-planes</ConceptLink> determined by its supporting <ConceptLink targetId="recta">lines</ConceptLink>: the half-plane with respect to $\overleftrightarrow{AB}$ containing $C$, the half-plane with respect to $\overleftrightarrow{BC}$ containing $A$, and the half-plane with respect to $\overleftrightarrow{CA}$ containing $B$. This region is a <ConceptLink targetId="conjunto-convexo">convex set</ConceptLink>.
    </PropiedadItem>

    <PropiedadItem 
      id="axioma-orden-4" 
      title="Pasch's Planar Intersection"
      formula="\ell \cap \operatorname{seg}(AB) \neq \emptyset \land C \notin \ell \implies (\ell \cap \operatorname{seg}(BC) \neq \emptyset \veebar \ell \cap \operatorname{seg}(CA) \neq \emptyset)"
    >
      By <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-orden-4">Axiom II.4</ConceptLink> (Pasch's Axiom), any coplanar line that intersects the interior of one side of a triangle and does not pass through any of its vertices necessarily intersects the interior of exactly one of the remaining two sides, ensuring the topological integrity of its <ConceptLink targetId="frontera">boundary</ConceptLink>.
    </PropiedadItem>
