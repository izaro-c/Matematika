<Capitular letra="A" /> triangle is the simplest <ConceptLink targetId="poligono">polygon</ConceptLink> in <ConceptLink targetId="geometria-euclidiana">planar geometry</ConceptLink> and serves as the structural foundation of <ConceptLink targetId="trigonometria">trigonometry</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="congruencia">congruence</ConceptLink> theory. Formed by connecting three non-<ConceptLink targetId="colinealidad">collinear</ConceptLink> <ConceptLink targetId="punto">points</ConceptLink>, it encloses a bounded <ConceptLink targetId="region-convexa">convex region</ConceptLink> in the <ConceptLink targetId="plano">plane</ConceptLink>.

<Definicion title="">
  Given three non-collinear points <VisualBind element="vertice-a" color="musgo">$A$</VisualBind>, <VisualBind element="vertice-b" color="musgo">$B$</VisualBind>, and <VisualBind element="vertice-c" color="musgo">$C$</VisualBind> belonging to a plane $\mathcal{P}$, the triangle <VisualBind element="triangulo" color="musgo">$\triangle ABC$</VisualBind> is the planar figure defined by the <ConceptLink targetId="union-conjuntos">union</ConceptLink> of the three line <ConceptLink targetId="segmento" isDependency={true}>segments</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="lado-ab" color="pavo">$\overline{AB}$</VisualBind>, <VisualBind element="lado-bc" color="terracota">$\overline{BC}$</VisualBind>, and <VisualBind element="lado-ca" color="ocre">$\overline{CA}$</VisualBind>:
  $$
  \triangle ABC = \overline{AB} \cup \overline{BC} \cup \overline{CA}
  $$
  The points $A, B, C$ are called <ConceptLink targetId="vertice">vertices</ConceptLink>, and the segments $\overline{AB}, \overline{BC}, \overline{CA}$ are the <ConceptLink targetId="lado">sides</ConceptLink> of the triangle.
</Definicion>

### Metric and angular classification

The metric characterization of triangles relies on <ConceptLink targetId="congruencia">congruence</ConceptLink> relationships among their <ConceptLink targetId="lado">sides</ConceptLink> and the magnitude of their interior <ConceptLink targetId="angulo">angles</ConceptLink>:

| Classification by sides | Geometric condition | Classification by angles | Angular condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo-equilatero">Equilateral</ConceptLink> | Three congruent sides ($\overline{AB} \cong \overline{BC} \cong \overline{CA}$) | <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo-acutangulo">Acute</ConceptLink> | Three <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-agudo">acute</ConceptLink> angles |
| <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo-isosceles">Isosceles</ConceptLink> | At least two congruent sides | <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo-rectangulo">Right</ConceptLink> | One <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-recto">right</ConceptLink> angle |
| <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo-escaleno">Scalene</ConceptLink> | No congruent pairs of sides | <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo-obtusangulo">Obtuse</ConceptLink> | One <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-obtuso">obtuse</ConceptLink> angle |

### Properties

- **<ConceptLink targetId="teorema-suma-angulos-triangulo">Angle sum</ConceptLink>**: In Euclidean geometry, the sum of the three interior angles of any triangle always equals $180^\circ$.
- **<ConceptLink targetId="teorema-desigualdad-triangular">Triangle inequality</ConceptLink>**: The length of any side is strictly less than the sum of the lengths of the other two sides.
- **<ConceptLink targetId="teorema-congruencia-ala">Congruence criteria</ConceptLink>**: The structural rigidity of a triangle allows equality to be established via SAS, ASA, and SSS criteria.
