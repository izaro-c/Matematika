This postulate complements <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-incidencia-4">Axiom I.4</ConceptLink>, completing the uniqueness property: three non-aligned points determine exactly one plane.

<Definicion title="Formal Statement">
  1. If three points <VisualBind element="pA" color="terracota">$A$</VisualBind>, <VisualBind element="pB" color="terracota">$B$</VisualBind>, and <VisualBind element="pC" color="terracota">$C$</VisualBind> are not <ConceptLink targetId="colinealidad">collinear</ConceptLink>, there exists at most one <ConceptLink targetId="plano">plane</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="plano" color="canela">$\pi$</VisualBind> containing them:
  $$
  \text{non-collinear}(A, B, C) \implies \exists! \pi \in \Pi \ (A \in \pi \land B \in \pi \land C \in \pi)
  $$
  2. Every plane $\pi \in \Pi$ contains at least three distinct non-collinear points.
</Definicion>

### Significance in the theory

Guarantees that planes are well-differentiated two-dimensional surfaces and do not duplicate across the same triangular references.
