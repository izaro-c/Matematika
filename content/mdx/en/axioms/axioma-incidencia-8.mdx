<Capitular letra="T" />here exist at least four <ConceptLink targetId="punto">points</ConceptLink> that do not belong to the same <ConceptLink targetId="plano">plane</ConceptLink>.

This postulate fixes the three-dimensional character of the geometry of <ConceptLink targetId="espacio">space</ConceptLink>, preventing the entire geometric universe from being confined to a single two-dimensional plane.

<Definicion title="Formal Statement">
  There exist at least four <ConceptLink targetId="punto">points</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="pA" color="terracota">$A$</VisualBind>, <VisualBind element="pB" color="terracota">$B$</VisualBind>, <VisualBind element="pC" color="terracota">$C$</VisualBind>, and <VisualBind element="pD" color="carbon">$D$</VisualBind> such that no <ConceptLink targetId="plano">plane</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="plano-pi" color="canela">$\pi$</VisualBind> contains all four simultaneously:
  $$
  \exists A, B, C, D \in \mathcal{P} \ \forall \pi \in \Pi \ \neg(A \in \pi \land B \in \pi \land C \in \pi \land D \in \pi)
  $$
</Definicion>

### Significance in the theory

- **<ConceptLink targetId="coplanaridad">Non-coplanarity</ConceptLink>:** Guarantees the existence of three-dimensional bodies such as the tetrahedron.
- **Closure of Group I:** This axiom concludes the incidence group, establishing the full spatial domain upon which order and congruence operate.
