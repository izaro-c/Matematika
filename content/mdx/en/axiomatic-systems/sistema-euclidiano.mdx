<Capitular letra="T" />he **Euclidean axiomatic system** is the <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-axiomatico">axiomatic system</ConceptLink> obtained by adding to <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-absoluto">absolute geometry</ConceptLink> <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-euclides">Axiom IV</ConceptLink> of <ConceptLink targetId="paralelas">parallels</ConceptLink>: through a <ConceptLink targetId="punto">point</ConceptLink> not on a <ConceptLink targetId="recta">line</ConceptLink> there passes, in the <ConceptLink targetId="plano">plane</ConceptLink> they determine, at most one parallel to it.

The fifth postulate of <ConceptLink targetId="euclides">Euclid</ConceptLink>'s <ConceptLink targetId="elementos-euclides">Elements</ConceptLink> was stated in terms of two lines cut by a <ConceptLink targetId="secante">transversal</ConceptLink>; <ConceptLink targetId="hilbert">Hilbert</ConceptLink> adopted the equivalent uniqueness form popularized by <ConceptLink targetId="playfair">John Playfair</ConceptLink> (1795). Since the existence of at least one parallel is already a <ConceptLink targetId="teorema">theorem</ConceptLink> of absolute geometry, Axiom IV only has to impose uniqueness.

<Definicion title="Formal structure">
  A **Euclidean structure** is an absolute geometry structure $\mathcal{S}_{\text{abs}} = (\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{L}, \Pi; \ \mathbf{I}, \ *, \ \cong_{\mathrm{seg}}, \ \cong_{\angle})$ that also satisfies <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-euclides">Axiom IV</ConceptLink>, so that $\mathcal{T}_{\text{euc}} = \mathrm{I} \cup \mathrm{II} \cup \mathrm{III} \cup \mathrm{IV} \cup \mathrm{V}$. Together with the absolute theorem on the existence of parallels, Axiom IV gives existence and uniqueness: for every plane $\pi \in \Pi$, every line $\ell \in \mathcal{L}$ with $\operatorname{tr}(\ell) \subseteq \operatorname{tr}(\pi)$ and every point $P \in \operatorname{tr}(\pi) \setminus \operatorname{tr}(\ell)$,
  $$
  \exists! \, m \in \mathcal{L} \ \bigl( \operatorname{tr}(m) \subseteq \operatorname{tr}(\pi) \land P \,\mathbf{I}\, m \land m \parallel \ell \bigr)
  $$
  where the <ConceptLink targetId="traza">trace</ConceptLink> expresses <ConceptLink targetId="coplanaridad">coplanarity</ConceptLink> and $\parallel$ is the (reflexive) <ConceptLink targetId="paralelas">parallelism</ConceptLink> between coplanar lines.
</Definicion>

### Characteristic theorems

Axiom IV distinguishes Euclidean from <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-hiperbolico">hyperbolic geometry</ConceptLink>:

- **Parallelism and angles**: for parallel lines cut by a transversal, the <ConceptLink targetId="angulos-alternos-internos">alternate interior angles</ConceptLink> are <ConceptLink targetId="congruencia">congruent</ConceptLink> (<ConceptLink targetId="teorema-angulos-alternos-internos">alternate interior angles theorem</ConceptLink>).
- **<ConceptLink targetId="relacion-transitiva">Transitivity</ConceptLink> of parallelism**: in a plane, two lines parallel to a third are parallel to each other; parallelism is an <ConceptLink targetId="relacion-equivalencia">equivalence relation</ConceptLink>.
- **Angle sum**: the <ConceptLink targetId="angulo">angles</ConceptLink> of every <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo">triangle</ConceptLink> sum to two <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-recto">right angles</ConceptLink> ($|\alpha| + |\beta| + |\gamma| = 180^\circ$, <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-suma-angulos-triangulo">triangle angle sum theorem</ConceptLink> with <ConceptLink targetId="medida-angular">angle measure</ConceptLink>).
- **Rectangles**: there exist <ConceptLink targetId="cuadrilatero">quadrilaterals</ConceptLink> with four right angles (<ConceptLink targetId="rectangulo">rectangles</ConceptLink>), the basis of <ConceptLink targetId="teoria-medida-area">area theory</ConceptLink>.
- **Similarity and proportion**: <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-tales">Thales' theorem</ConceptLink> and the existence of figures with non-congruent <ConceptLink targetId="semejanza">similarity</ConceptLink>.
- **<ConceptLink targetId="teorema-pitagoras">Pythagorean theorem</ConceptLink>**: in a <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo-rectangulo">right triangle</ConceptLink> $\triangle ABC$ with right angle at $C$, and with <ConceptLink targetId="longitud">lengths</ConceptLink> $a = |BC|$, $b = |CA|$ and $c = |AB|$, $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.

### Model, consistency and categoricity

The canonical model is Cartesian space $\mathbb{R}^3$ (<ConceptLink targetId="modelo-cartesiano">Cartesian model</ConceptLink>), in which points are triples of <ConceptLink targetId="numero-real">real numbers</ConceptLink>, lines and planes are described linearly and congruence is defined with Euclidean <ConceptLink targetId="distancia">distance</ConceptLink> ($\mathbb{R}^2$ in the plane). Hilbert constructs these analytic models over a number <ConceptLink targetId="cuerpo">field</ConceptLink> and proves the relative <ConceptLink targetId="consistencia-logica">consistency</ConceptLink> of the system.

With the <ConceptLink targetId="continuidad">continuity axioms</ConceptLink> V.1 and V.2 the system is <ConceptLink targetId="categoricidad">categorical</ConceptLink>: all its models are <ConceptLink targetId="isomorfismo">isomorphic</ConceptLink> to $\mathbb{R}^3$. Without <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-completitud">Axiom V.2</ConceptLink>, by contrast, it admits non-isomorphic models, such as the space built over the field of real algebraic numbers.
