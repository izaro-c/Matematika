<Capitular letra="T" />he **absolute geometry system**, also called *neutral geometry*, is the <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-axiomatico">axiomatic system</ConceptLink> formed by groups I (<ConceptLink targetId="sistema-incidencia">incidence</ConceptLink>), II (<ConceptLink targetId="estar-entre">order</ConceptLink>), III (<ConceptLink targetId="congruencia">congruence</ConceptLink>) and V (<ConceptLink targetId="continuidad">continuity</ConceptLink>) of <ConceptLink targetId="hilbert">David Hilbert</ConceptLink>, with no <ConceptLink targetId="axioma">axiom</ConceptLink> about <ConceptLink targetId="paralelas">parallels</ConceptLink>. Every <ConceptLink targetId="teorema">theorem</ConceptLink> proved in it holds both in <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-euclidiano">Euclidean geometry</ConceptLink> and in <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-hiperbolico">hyperbolic geometry</ConceptLink>.

The name comes from <ConceptLink targetId="bolyai">János Bolyai</ConceptLink>, who in his *Appendix* (1832) called the part of geometry that does not depend on <ConceptLink targetId="euclides">Euclid</ConceptLink>'s fifth postulate the "absolute science of space". <ConceptLink targetId="lobachevski">Nikolai Lobachevsky</ConceptLink> developed the same part as the basis of his geometry, and Hilbert gave it a complete axiomatic form in the *Grundlagen der Geometrie*.

<Definicion title="Formal structure">
  An **absolute geometry structure** is a <ConceptLink targetId="estructura-geometrica">structure</ConceptLink> over Hilbert's <ConceptLink targetId="signatura">signature</ConceptLink>
  $$
  \mathcal{S}_{\text{abs}} = (\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{L}, \Pi; \ \mathbf{I}, \ *, \ \cong_{\mathrm{seg}}, \ \cong_{\angle})
  $$
  with <ConceptLink targetId="conjunto-disjunto">disjoint</ConceptLink> <ConceptLink targetId="dominio">domains</ConceptLink> of <ConceptLink targetId="punto">points</ConceptLink> $\mathcal{P}$, <ConceptLink targetId="recta">lines</ConceptLink> $\mathcal{L}$ and <ConceptLink targetId="plano">planes</ConceptLink> $\Pi$, the <ConceptLink targetId="incidencia">incidence</ConceptLink> relation $\mathbf{I}$, the ternary <ConceptLink targetId="estar-entre">betweenness</ConceptLink> relation ($A * B * C$) and the <ConceptLink targetId="congruencia">congruence</ConceptLink> relations for <ConceptLink targetId="segmento">segments</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="angulo">angles</ConceptLink>, which satisfies the theory
  $$

### Groups of axioms

1. **Incidence (I.1–I.8)**: from <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-incidencia-1">Axiom I.1</ConceptLink> to <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-incidencia-8">Axiom I.8</ConceptLink>. The first three are planar; from <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-incidencia-4">Axiom I.4</ConceptLink> onwards they govern planes and the three-dimensionality of <ConceptLink targetId="espacio">space</ConceptLink>.
2. **Order (II.1–II.4)**: from <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-orden-1">Axiom II.1</ConceptLink> to <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-orden-4">Axiom II.4</ConceptLink> (<ConceptLink targetId="pasch">Pasch</ConceptLink>'s axiom). They govern the relation $A * B * C$ and allow one to define <ConceptLink targetId="segmento">segments</ConceptLink>, <ConceptLink targetId="semirrecta">rays</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="semiplano">half-planes</ConceptLink>.
3. **Congruence (III.1–III.5)**: from <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-congruencia-1">Axiom III.1</ConceptLink> to <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-congruencia-5">Axiom III.5</ConceptLink>. They govern the transport of segments and angles and culminate in the part of the SAS criterion postulated by Axiom III.5.
4. **Continuity (V.1–V.2)**: <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-arquimedes">Axiom V.1 (Archimedes)</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-completitud">Axiom V.2 (line completeness)</ConceptLink>. Together they allow one to assign to each segment a <ConceptLink targetId="longitud">length</ConceptLink> $|AB| \in \mathbb{R}$ and to each angle an <ConceptLink targetId="medida-angular">angle measure</ConceptLink>.

### Theorems of absolute geometry

Many classical results need only groups I–III, that is, they hold even without continuity axioms:

- The <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-triangulo-isosceles">isosceles triangle theorem</ConceptLink> and the <ConceptLink targetId="congruencia-triangulos">triangle congruence</ConceptLink> criteria <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-congruencia-lal">SAS</ConceptLink>, <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-congruencia-ala">ASA</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-congruencia-lll">SSS</ConceptLink>.
- The <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-angulo-exterior">exterior angle theorem</ConceptLink>: in every <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo">triangle</ConceptLink>, each <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-exterior">exterior angle</ConceptLink> is greater, in the sense of the <ConceptLink targetId="orden-angulos">ordering of angles</ConceptLink>, than each of the two interior angles not <ConceptLink targetId="angulos-adyacentes">adjacent</ConceptLink> to it.
- The existence of parallels: if a <ConceptLink targetId="secante">transversal</ConceptLink> makes congruent <ConceptLink targetId="angulos-alternos-internos">alternate interior angles</ConceptLink> with two lines, those lines are parallel (the absolute direction of the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-angulos-alternos-internos">alternate interior angles theorem</ConceptLink>). Consequently, through every point $P$ not on a line $\ell$ there passes at least one parallel to $\ell$: the <ConceptLink targetId="perpendicular">perpendicular</ConceptLink> at $P$ to the perpendicular dropped from $P$ to $\ell$. The two lines thus have a <ConceptLink targetId="perpendicular-comun">common perpendicular</ConceptLink> (double perpendicular construction).

Other results use continuity. The <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-saccheri-legendre">Saccheri–Legendre theorem</ConceptLink> states that the sum of the angle measures of every triangle satisfies
$$
|\alpha| + |\beta| + |\gamma| \le 180^\circ
$$
and its proof uses <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-arquimedes">Axiom V.1</ConceptLink>. Without the Archimedean axiom there are (non-Archimedean) geometries satisfying groups I–III with triangles whose angle sum exceeds two <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-recto">right angles</ConceptLink>.

### Extensions, models and consistency

Absolute geometry does not decide whether one or more than one parallel passes through a point not on a line. Adding <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-euclides">Axiom IV</ConceptLink> yields the <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-euclidiano">Euclidean system</ConceptLink>; adding the <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-hiperbolico">hyperbolic parallel axiom</ConceptLink> yields the <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-hiperbolico">hyperbolic system</ConceptLink>. With the continuity axioms these are the only two possibilities: either every triangle has angle sum equal to two right angles, or every triangle has a smaller one.

Since the system contains the spatial axioms I.4–I.8, its models are three-dimensional: Cartesian space $\mathbb{R}^3$ (<ConceptLink targetId="modelo-cartesiano">Cartesian model</ConceptLink>) and the Poincaré or Klein ball in $\mathbb{R}^3$, the spatial version of the <ConceptLink targetId="modelo-poincare">Poincaré disk</ConceptLink>. The planar versions $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $\mathbb{D}^2$ satisfy only the planar axioms (I.1–I.3 and groups II, III and V restricted to a plane). The existence of these models proves the <ConceptLink targetId="consistencia-logica">consistency</ConceptLink> of the system relative to the arithmetic of $\mathbb{R}$, and that neither Axiom IV nor its negation can be deduced from it (<ConceptLink targetId="independencia-axiomatica">independence</ConceptLink> of the parallel axiom).
