<Capitular letra="P" />avel Samuilovich Urysohn (1898–1924) was a Russian mathematician born in Odessa. He entered the University of Moscow in 1915 to study physics, but the lectures of <ConceptLink targetId="luzin">Nikolai Luzin</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="egorov">Dmitri Egorov</ConceptLink> drew him to mathematics. In barely three years of research he made major contributions to general <ConceptLink targetId="topologia">topology</ConceptLink>. He drowned at the age of 26 at Batz-sur-Mer, on the coast of Brittany.

<Separador />

### Main Contributions

- **Dimension theory**: Starting from a question of Egorov about the intrinsic definition of curve and surface, in 1921 he conceived an inductive definition of <ConceptLink targetId="dimension">dimension</ConceptLink> based on the dimension of the <ConceptLink targetId="frontera">boundary</ConceptLink> of small neighbourhoods. He published notes in 1922 and wrote in 1923 his great memoir, which appeared posthumously in *Fundamenta Mathematicae* (1925–1926). <ConceptLink targetId="menger">Karl Menger</ConceptLink> arrived independently at the same definition.
- **<ConceptLink targetId="lema">Urysohn's lemma</ConceptLink>**: In a normal <ConceptLink targetId="espacio">space</ConceptLink>, two <ConceptLink targetId="conjunto-disjunto">disjoint</ConceptLink> closed sets can be separated by a continuous <ConceptLink targetId="funcion">function</ConceptLink> taking the value $0$ on one and $1$ on the other. It is the basic tool for constructing continuous functions on topological spaces.
- **Metrization**: He proved that every normal space with a countable base is metrizable, the first major metrization theorem, and constructed a universal separable <ConceptLink targetId="espacio-metrico">metric space</ConceptLink> containing an isometric copy of every other.
- **Compact spaces**: With <ConceptLink targetId="alexandrov">Pavel Alexandrov</ConceptLink> he developed the theory of compact topological spaces.

<Separador />

### Influence and Legacy

Alexandrov and Brouwer took care of publishing his posthumous work. His definition of dimension, equivalent to Menger's, became, together with the covering dimension of <ConceptLink targetId="lebesgue">Henri Lebesgue</ConceptLink>, the basis of the theory set out by <ConceptLink targetId="hurewicz">Witold Hurewicz</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="wallman">Henry Wallman</ConceptLink>.
