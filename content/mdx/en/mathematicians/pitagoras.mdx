<Capitular letra="P" />ythagoras of Samos (c. 570 – c. 495 BC) was a Greek philosopher who around 530 BC settled in Croton, in Magna Graecia, where he founded a philosophical and religious community. He left no writings, and testimony about him comes from later authors. It is therefore difficult to separate his ideas from those of his disciples. The attributions below reflect the tradition transmitted by <ConceptLink targetId="proclo">Proclus</ConceptLink> from the history of geometry of <ConceptLink targetId="eudemo">Eudemus of Rhodes</ConceptLink>.

<Separador />

### Attributed Contributions

- **The <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-pitagoras">Pythagorean theorem</ConceptLink>**: In every <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo-rectangulo">right triangle</ConceptLink>, the square built on the <ConceptLink targetId="hipotenusa">hypotenuse</ConceptLink> equals the sum of the squares built on the <ConceptLink targetId="cateto">legs</ConceptLink>: $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, where $a$ and $b$ are the <ConceptLink targetId="longitud">lengths</ConceptLink> of the legs and $c$ that of the hypotenuse. The relation was known in Babylon more than a millennium earlier (tablet Plimpton 322) and appears in the Indian *Śulbasūtras*. Tradition attributes a general proof to Pythagoras or his school, but none survives; the oldest known is the one in the <ConceptLink targetId="elementos-euclides">*Elements*</ConceptLink> of <ConceptLink targetId="euclides">Euclid</ConceptLink> (I.47).
- **Incommensurability**: The discovery that the <ConceptLink targetId="diagonal">diagonal</ConceptLink> and the <ConceptLink targetId="lado">side</ConceptLink> of a <ConceptLink targetId="cuadrado">square</ConceptLink> have no common measure, that is, that $\sqrt{2}$ is not a quotient of two <ConceptLink targetId="numero-entero">integers</ConceptLink>, is attributed to the Pythagorean school of the 5th century BC, and traditionally to <ConceptLink targetId="hipaso">Hippasus of Metapontum</ConceptLink>.
- **Music and proportion**: The Pythagoreans are credited with the observation that consonant <ConceptLink targetId="intervalo">intervals</ConceptLink> correspond to simple ratios of string lengths: octave ($2:1$), fifth ($3:2$) and fourth ($4:3$).
- **Figurate numbers**: The school studied triangular, square and oblong numbers as configurations of <ConceptLink targetId="punto">points</ConceptLink>, linking arithmetic and geometry.

### Influence and Legacy

According to <ConceptLink targetId="aristoteles">Aristotle</ConceptLink>, the Pythagoreans held that the principles of numbers are the principles of all things, an idea summed up in the motto "all is number". This conception influenced <ConceptLink targetId="platon">Plato</ConceptLink> and, through him, the tradition that culminated in Euclid's *Elements*; centuries later it inspired the search for mathematical laws of nature in <ConceptLink targetId="kepler">Johannes Kepler</ConceptLink>.

### Sources

No work by Pythagoras survives. The main sources are Aristotle, Proclus' commentary on Book I of the *Elements*, and the late biographies by Diogenes Laërtius, Porphyry and Iamblichus (3rd–4th centuries AD).
