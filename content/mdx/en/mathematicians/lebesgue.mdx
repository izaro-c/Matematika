<Capitular letra="H" />enri Lebesgue (1875–1941) was a French mathematician born in Beauvais who died in Paris. He studied at the École Normale Supérieure, taught in Rennes, Poitiers and at the Sorbonne, and from 1921 was professor at the Collège de France; in 1922 he was elected to the Academy of Sciences. His theory of integration transformed 20th-century analysis.

<Separador />

### Main Contributions

- **Lebesgue measure and integral**: In his thesis *Intégrale, longueur, aire* (1902) he defined a <ConceptLink targetId="medida">measure</ConceptLink> for a broad class of sets of <ConceptLink targetId="numero-real">real numbers</ConceptLink>, extending the <ConceptLink targetId="longitud">length</ConceptLink> of <ConceptLink targetId="intervalo">intervals</ConceptLink>, and based on it an integral that groups the values of the <ConceptLink targetId="funcion">function</ConceptLink> instead of subdividing its <ConceptLink targetId="dominio">domain</ConceptLink>. The Lebesgue integral behaves well under passage to the <ConceptLink targetId="limite">limit</ConceptLink> (<ConceptLink targetId="teorema-convergencia-dominada">dominated convergence theorem</ConceptLink>) and is the basis of modern probability theory and functional analysis.
- **Trigonometric series**: He applied his integral to the study of <ConceptLink targetId="serie-fourier">Fourier series</ConceptLink>, in particular to convergence and to the uniqueness of the coefficients.
- **Covering dimension**: In 1911 he proposed to prove the invariance of <ConceptLink targetId="dimension">dimension</ConceptLink> through a property of coverings: an $n$-dimensional cube can be covered by arbitrarily small closed sets so that no <ConceptLink targetId="punto">point</ConceptLink> belongs to more than $n + 1$ of them, but not with $n$. His proof was incomplete and was completed by <ConceptLink targetId="brouwer">L. E. J. Brouwer</ConceptLink> in 1913. This idea is the origin of covering dimension, which <ConceptLink targetId="cech">Eduard Čech</ConceptLink> formalized for general topological <ConceptLink targetId="espacio">spaces</ConceptLink> in 1933.

### Influence and Legacy

The Lebesgue integral replaced that of <ConceptLink targetId="riemann">Bernhard Riemann</ConceptLink> as the standard tool of analysis. His covering principle, together with the inductive definitions of <ConceptLink targetId="menger">Karl Menger</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="urysohn">Pavel Urysohn</ConceptLink>, is one of the foundations of the dimension theory set out by <ConceptLink targetId="hurewicz">Witold Hurewicz</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="wallman">Henry Wallman</ConceptLink>. Lebesgue also devoted his last years to didactic writings on the measurement of magnitudes and geometric constructions.
