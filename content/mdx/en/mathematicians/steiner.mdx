<Capitular letra="J" />akob Steiner (1796–1863) was a Swiss mathematician born in Utzenstorf and died in Bern. The son of peasants, he learned to read and write at the age of fourteen; in 1814 he entered Pestalozzi's school in Yverdon and later studied in Heidelberg and Berlin. A contributor from its very first volume (1826) to the journal of <ConceptLink targetId="crelle">August Leopold Crelle</ConceptLink>, in 1834 an extraordinary chair of geometry was created for him at the University of Berlin, which he held until his death.

<Separador />

### Main Contributions

- **Synthetic projective geometry**: In *Systematische Entwicklung der Abhängigkeit geometrischer Gestalten voneinander* (1832) he sought to derive figures from one another through projective correspondences between pencils and ranges of points. His theorem on generating conics as the locus of intersections of two projective pencils of lines is one of the central results of synthetic <ConceptLink targetId="geometria-proyectiva">projective geometry</ConceptLink>.
- **Constructions with straightedge and a fixed circle**: In *Die geometrischen Constructionen, ausgeführt mittelst der geraden Linie und eines festen Kreises* (1833) he proved that every ruler-and-compass construction can be performed with the straightedge alone if a fixed circle with its center is available (the Poncelet–Steiner theorem, conjectured by <ConceptLink targetId="poncelet">Jean-Victor Poncelet</ConceptLink>).
- **Isoperimetric problems**: He provided geometric arguments, using symmetrization, that the circle encloses the greatest area among figures of equal perimeter; his reasoning assumed the existence of a maximum, which <ConceptLink targetId="weierstrass">Karl Weierstrass</ConceptLink> later justified rigorously.
- **Other results**: He studied the Steiner surface (or Roman surface), the inscribed and circumscribed Steiner ellipses of a <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo">triangle</ConceptLink>, the 28 bitangents of a plane quartic, and Steiner systems in combinatorics (1853).

<Separador />

### Influence and Legacy

Steiner rejected algebra and analysis in geometry, convinced that calculation replaces thinking, whereas geometry stimulates it. He was considered by his contemporaries the greatest geometer since <ConceptLink targetId="apolonio">Apollonius of Perga</ConceptLink>. Together with Poncelet and <ConceptLink targetId="staudt">Karl von Staudt</ConceptLink>, he gave <ConceptLink targetId="geometria-sintetica">synthetic geometry</ConceptLink> the momentum that culminated in the axiomatic foundations of the late 19th century.
