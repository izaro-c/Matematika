<Capitular letra="T" />hales of Miletus (c. 624 – c. 546 BC) was an Ionian philosopher, astronomer and geometer. <ConceptLink targetId="aristoteles">Aristotle</ConceptLink> regarded him as the founder of natural philosophy, and Herodotus credits him with predicting the eclipse of 585 BC. He left no writings: what we know of his geometry comes from much later authors, especially <ConceptLink targetId="proclo">Proclus</ConceptLink> (5th century AD), who cites the history of geometry of <ConceptLink targetId="eudemo">Eudemus of Rhodes</ConceptLink>, and Diogenes Laërtius. His dates are approximate.

### Attributed Contributions

According to Proclus, following Eudemus, Thales knew or proved the following results, which later appear in the <ConceptLink targetId="elementos-euclides">*Elements*</ConceptLink>:

- Every <ConceptLink targetId="diametro">diameter</ConceptLink> bisects the circle.
- The base angles of an <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo-isosceles">isosceles triangle</ConceptLink> are equal (<ConceptLink targetId="teorema-triangulo-isosceles">isosceles triangle theorem</ConceptLink>).
- <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-angulos-opuestos-verticales">Vertical angles</ConceptLink> are equal; Eudemus specifies that Thales discovered it, but that the scientific proof is due to <ConceptLink targetId="euclides">Euclid</ConceptLink>.
- Two <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo">triangles</ConceptLink> having one <ConceptLink targetId="lado">side</ConceptLink> and the two adjacent angles equal are equal (<ConceptLink targetId="teorema-congruencia-ala">ASA criterion</ConceptLink>); Eudemus infers that Thales used it to compute the <ConceptLink targetId="distancia">distance</ConceptLink> of ships at sea.

Diogenes Laërtius also credits him with the fact that every angle inscribed in a semicircle is a <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-recto">right angle</ConceptLink> (<ConceptLink targetId="teorema-angulo-inscrito">inscribed angle theorem</ConceptLink>), although other sources attribute it to <ConceptLink targetId="pitagoras">Pythagoras</ConceptLink>, and with measuring the height of the pyramids by comparing their shadows with that of an object of known height.

### The "Thales' theorem"

In the school tradition of Spanish, French and Italian, the name <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-tales">Thales' theorem</ConceptLink> refers to the proportionality of the <ConceptLink targetId="segmento">segments</ConceptLink> cut by <ConceptLink targetId="paralelas">parallel</ConceptLink> lines on two <ConceptLink targetId="secante">transversals</ConceptLink> (*Elements* VI.2), the basis of the theory of <ConceptLink targetId="semejanza">similarity</ConceptLink>; in English it is usually called the intercept theorem. No ancient source attributes this result to Thales; the name is modern. In the German and English traditions, by contrast, "Thales' theorem" is the one about the angle inscribed in a semicircle.

### Influence and Legacy

The Greek tradition saw in Thales the first to justify general geometric results instead of merely using computational rules, such as those of Egypt and Babylon. Whatever the real scope of his proofs, this image made him the starting point of the history of deductive geometry that leads to Pythagoras and Euclid.
