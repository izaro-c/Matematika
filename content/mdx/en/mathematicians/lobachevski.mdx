<Capitular letra="N" />ikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky (1792–1856) was a Russian mathematician born in Nizhny Novgorod. He studied and taught at the University of Kazan, of which he was rector from 1827 to 1846. He was the first to publish a systematic theory of <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-hiperbolico">hyperbolic geometry</ConceptLink>, developed independently of <ConceptLink targetId="bolyai">János Bolyai</ConceptLink> and of <ConceptLink targetId="gauss">Carl Friedrich Gauss</ConceptLink>.

<Separador />

### Main Contributions

- **Hyperbolic geometry**: In February 1826 he presented in Kazan an account, now lost, of a geometry in which the <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-euclides">parallel postulate</ConceptLink> is replaced by the <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-hiperbolico">hyperbolic axiom</ConceptLink>: through a <ConceptLink targetId="punto">point</ConceptLink> not on a <ConceptLink targetId="recta">line</ConceptLink> there pass at least two lines that do not meet it. The first publication was the memoir *On the Principles of Geometry* (1829–1830), in the *Kazan Messenger*.
- **Hyperbolic trigonometry and angle of parallelism**: He introduced the <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-paralelismo">angle of parallelism</ConceptLink> $\theta(d)$, which depends on the distance $d$ from the point to the line, obtained the formulas of hyperbolic trigonometry, showed that Euclidean geometry holds on the <ConceptLink targetId="horosfera">horosphere</ConceptLink>, and computed hyperbolic areas and volumes.
- **The question of consistency**: Lobachevsky deduced many consequences without finding a contradiction and observed that his trigonometric formulas are those of spherical trigonometry with an imaginary radius, but he did not prove the <ConceptLink targetId="consistencia-logica">consistency</ConceptLink> of his geometry. Relative consistency was established later with the models of <ConceptLink targetId="beltrami">Eugenio Beltrami</ConceptLink> (1868), <ConceptLink targetId="klein">Felix Klein</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="poincare">Henri Poincaré</ConceptLink>.
- **Analysis**: He gave a method for approximating roots of polynomials, also discovered by <ConceptLink targetId="dandelin">Germinal Pierre Dandelin</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="graffe">Karl Heinrich Gräffe</ConceptLink>, and worked on trigonometric series and the notion of function.

<Separador />

### Influence and Legacy

His work was ignored or ridiculed in Russia during his lifetime; Gauss, who read it in German, appreciated it privately. <ConceptLink targetId="clifford">William Kingdon Clifford</ConceptLink> called him "the Copernicus of Geometry". The acceptance of hyperbolic geometry in the second half of the 19th century showed that <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-euclidiano">Euclidean geometry</ConceptLink> is not the only coherent theory of space.

<Separador />

### Notable Works

- *On the Principles of Geometry* (1829–1830) — First publication of hyperbolic geometry.
- *Imaginary Geometry* (in Russian, 1835; French version *Géométrie imaginaire*, 1837) — Analytic exposition of the new system.
- *New Principles of Geometry with a Complete Theory of Parallels* (1835–1838).
- *Geometrische Untersuchungen zur Theorie der Parallellinien* (1840) — Short account in German, the one Gauss read.
- *Pangeometry* (1855; French version, 1856) — Final synthesis, dictated when he was already blind.
