<Capitular letra="E" />uclid of Alexandria (fl. c. 300 BC) was a Greek mathematician who taught in Alexandria during the reign of Ptolemy I. Almost nothing is known of his life: his dates of birth and death are conjectural, and the biographical information comes from much later authors such as <ConceptLink targetId="proclo">Proclus</ConceptLink> (5th century AD). His main work, the <ConceptLink targetId="elementos-euclides">*Elements*</ConceptLink>, was the model of deductive reasoning for more than two millennia.

<Separador />

### Main Contributions

- **The *Elements* (13 books)**: They systematize earlier Greek geometry and arithmetic, largely derived from <ConceptLink targetId="eudoxo">Eudoxus</ConceptLink>, <ConceptLink targetId="teeteto">Theaetetus</ConceptLink> and the <ConceptLink targetId="pitagoras">Pythagorean</ConceptLink> tradition:
  - Books I–IV: plane geometry, straightedge-and-compass constructions, <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo">triangles</ConceptLink>, <ConceptLink targetId="paralelas">parallel lines</ConceptLink>, the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-pitagoras">Pythagorean theorem</ConceptLink> (I.47) and its converse (I.48).
  - Books V–VI: Eudoxus' theory of proportions and <ConceptLink targetId="semejanza">similar</ConceptLink> figures, with the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-tales">intercept theorem</ConceptLink> (VI.2).
  - Books VII–IX: arithmetic, with Euclid's algorithm for the greatest common divisor (VII.1–2), the infinitude of the primes (IX.20) and the even perfect numbers (IX.36).
  - Book X: classification of incommensurable magnitudes.
  - Books XI–XIII: solid geometry, the <ConceptLink targetId="metodo-exhaucion">method of exhaustion</ConceptLink> (Book XII) and the construction of the five regular <ConceptLink targetId="poliedro">polyhedra</ConceptLink> (Book XIII).
- **The axiomatic method**: Book I starts from 23 definitions, 5 postulates and 5 common notions, among them the <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-euclides">parallel postulate</ConceptLink>. Several proofs rely on unstated assumptions, such as the existence of intersection points of circles or order properties of the <ConceptLink targetId="recta">line</ConceptLink>, which <ConceptLink targetId="pasch">Moritz Pasch</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="hilbert">David Hilbert</ConceptLink> turned into explicit axioms in the 19th century.

<Separador />

### Influence and Legacy

The *Elements* were copied, translated and commented upon without interruption from Antiquity, through the medieval Arabic and Latin versions, and served as a textbook until the 19th century. Attempts to deduce the fifth postulate from the others led to the <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-hiperbolico">hyperbolic geometry</ConceptLink> of <ConceptLink targetId="bolyai">János Bolyai</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="lobachevski">Nikolai Lobachevsky</ConceptLink>, whose relative consistency was established by means of models. Hilbert's <ConceptLink targetId="fundamentos-geometria-hilbert">*Foundations of Geometry*</ConceptLink> (1899) completed the modern axiomatization of <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-euclidiano">Euclidean geometry</ConceptLink>.

### Notable Works

- <ConceptLink targetId="elementos-euclides">*Elements*</ConceptLink> — Treatise on geometry and arithmetic in 13 books.
- *Data* — Propositions on which elements of a figure are determined by others.
- *Optics* and *Phaenomena* — Treatises on geometric optics and spherical astronomy.
- Lost works: *Porisms*, *Conics*, *Surface Loci*.
