<Capitular letra="T" />he **Poincaré disk** is an analytic model of the hyperbolic <ConceptLink targetId="plano">plane</ConceptLink>. Its <ConceptLink targetId="punto">points</ConceptLink> are those of the open unit disk $\mathbb{D}^2$, and its <ConceptLink targetId="recta">lines</ConceptLink> are the <ConceptLink targetId="diametro">diameters</ConceptLink> and the <ConceptLink targetId="circunferencia">circle</ConceptLink> arcs orthogonal to the boundary. It satisfies the planar axioms of the <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-hiperbolico">hyperbolic system</ConceptLink> (I.1–I.3, II, III, the <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-hiperbolico">hyperbolic parallel axiom</ConceptLink> and V).

Since it is built inside <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-euclidiano">Euclidean geometry</ConceptLink>, the model proves that plane hyperbolic geometry is <ConceptLink targetId="consistencia-logica">consistent</ConceptLink> if Euclidean geometry is. <ConceptLink targetId="poincare">Henri Poincaré</ConceptLink> introduced it in 1882, after the models of <ConceptLink targetId="beltrami">Eugenio Beltrami</ConceptLink> (1868) and <ConceptLink targetId="klein">Felix Klein</ConceptLink> (1871). It is <ConceptLink targetId="aplicacion-conforme">conformal</ConceptLink>: the measure of hyperbolic angles coincides with that of the Euclidean angles formed by the curves.

<Definicion title="Structure of the model">
  The model is the <ConceptLink targetId="estructura-geometrica">structure</ConceptLink> $\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{L}; \ \mathbf{I}, \ *, \ \cong_{\mathrm{seg}}, \ \cong_{\angle})$ defined as follows:

  - **Points**: $\mathcal{P} = \mathbb{D}^2 = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid x^2 + y^2 < 1\}$. The points of the <VisualBind element="frontera" color="carbon">boundary circle $\partial \mathbb{D}^2$</VisualBind> do not belong to the model; they are called *ideal points*.
  - **Lines**: the intersections with $\mathbb{D}^2$ of the diameters of the disk and of the Euclidean circles that meet $\partial \mathbb{D}^2$ <ConceptLink targetId="perpendicular">perpendicularly</ConceptLink>, such as the line <VisualBind element="geodesica-principal" color="terracota">$\ell$</VisualBind>. <ConceptLink targetId="incidencia">Incidence</ConceptLink> is membership in that arc or diameter.
  - **<ConceptLink targetId="estar-entre">Betweenness</ConceptLink>**: $A * B * C$ if $A$, $B$ and $C$ are distinct points of the same line and $B$ lies on the Euclidean arc with endpoints $A$ and $C$.
  - **<ConceptLink targetId="congruencia">Congruence</ConceptLink>**: $\overline{AB} \cong \overline{CD}$ if $d(A, B) = d(C, D)$ for the hyperbolic <ConceptLink targetId="distancia">distance</ConceptLink>
    $$
    d(A, B) = \operatorname{arcosh}\!\left( 1 + \frac{2\,\lVert A - B \rVert^2}{(1 - \lVert A \rVert^2)(1 - \lVert B \rVert^2)} \right)
    $$
    and two <ConceptLink targetId="angulo">angles</ConceptLink> are congruent if the Euclidean angles between the tangents to their <ConceptLink targetId="lado">sides</ConceptLink> have the same measure.

  The distance comes from the <ConceptLink targetId="longitud">length</ConceptLink> element $ds = \dfrac{2\sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}}{1 - (x^2 + y^2)}$; as a point approaches the boundary, its distance to any fixed point of the disk tends to $+\infty$.
</Definicion>

### Parallelism in the model

Let <VisualBind element="geodesica-principal" color="terracota">$\ell$</VisualBind> be a line and <VisualBind element="P" color="terracota">$P$</VisualBind> a point not incident with it. The lines through $P$ fall into three classes:

- those meeting $\ell$ at a point of $\mathbb{D}^2$;
- two <ConceptLink targetId="paralelas-limite">limiting parallels</ConceptLink>, such as <VisualBind element="limite1" color="ocre">the one shown in the figure</VisualBind>, which share an ideal point of $\partial \mathbb{D}^2$ with $\ell$ but no point of the model;
- infinitely many <ConceptLink targetId="ultraparalelas">ultraparallels</ConceptLink>, such as <VisualBind element="ultra1" color="canela">this one</VisualBind>, which have no point in common with $\ell$ either in $\mathbb{D}^2$ or in $\partial \mathbb{D}^2$, and have a unique <ConceptLink targetId="perpendicular-comun">common perpendicular</ConceptLink> with it.

Limiting parallels and ultraparallels are <ConceptLink targetId="paralelas">parallel</ConceptLink> to $\ell$, so infinitely many parallels to $\ell$ pass through $P$.

  <PropiedadesGrupo title="Satisfaction of the axioms of the hyperbolic system">
    <PropiedadItem
      id="poincare-incidencia"
      title="Planar incidence axioms"
    >
      Through two distinct points of $\mathbb{D}^2$ there passes a unique diameter or a unique circle orthogonal to $\partial \mathbb{D}^2$ (<ConceptLink targetId="axioma-incidencia-1">Axiom I.1</ConceptLink>); every line has infinitely many points (<ConceptLink targetId="axioma-incidencia-2">Axiom I.2</ConceptLink>), and there are three non-<ConceptLink targetId="colinealidad">collinear</ConceptLink> points (<ConceptLink targetId="axioma-incidencia-3">Axiom I.3</ConceptLink>).
    </PropiedadItem>

    <PropiedadItem
      id="poincare-orden"
      title="Order axioms"
    >
      Each line is an open arc ordered like an <ConceptLink targetId="intervalo">interval</ConceptLink> of $\mathbb{R}$, which yields axioms <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-orden-1">II.1</ConceptLink>–<ConceptLink targetId="axioma-orden-3">II.3</ConceptLink>; each line separates the disk into two regions, the <ConceptLink targetId="semiplano">half-planes</ConceptLink>, and <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-orden-4">Pasch's axiom</ConceptLink> holds.
    </PropiedadItem>

    <PropiedadItem
      id="poincare-congruencia"
      title="Congruence axioms"
      formula="z \longmapsto e^{i\varphi}\,\frac{z - a}{1 - \bar{a}\,z}, \quad |a| < 1"
    >
      The Möbius transformations above (and their compositions with conjugation) are the <ConceptLink targetId="isometria">isometries</ConceptLink> of the model: they preserve $d$ and angles, and act transitively on points and directions. They transport <ConceptLink targetId="segmento">segments</ConceptLink> and angles (<ConceptLink targetId="axioma-congruencia-1">Axiom III.1</ConceptLink>, <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-congruencia-4">Axiom III.4</ConceptLink>), and <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-congruencia-2">III.2</ConceptLink>, <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-congruencia-3">III.3</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-congruencia-5">III.5</ConceptLink> are verified.
    </PropiedadItem>

    <PropiedadItem
      id="poincare-paralelas"
      title="Hyperbolic parallel axiom"
    >
      As shown above, infinitely many parallels to a line pass through a point not on it; in particular at least two, which satisfies the <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-hiperbolico">hyperbolic parallel axiom</ConceptLink> and refutes <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-euclides">Axiom IV</ConceptLink>.
    </PropiedadItem>

    <PropiedadItem
      id="poincare-continuidad"
      title="Continuity axioms"
    >
      Hyperbolic distance measured from a fixed point of a line establishes an order-preserving <ConceptLink targetId="biyeccion">bijection</ConceptLink> between that line and $\mathbb{R}$; the Archimedean property and completeness of $\mathbb{R}$ yield <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-arquimedes">Axiom V.1</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-completitud">Axiom V.2</ConceptLink>.
    </PropiedadItem>
  </PropiedadesGrupo>

  <PropiedadesGrupo title="Structural and metric properties">
    <PropiedadItem
      id="poincare-area-defecto"
      title="Area and angular defect"
      formula="\operatorname{A}(\triangle ABC) = \pi - (\alpha + \beta + \gamma)"
    >
      If $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are the radian measures of the angles of a <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo">triangle</ConceptLink>, their sum is less than $\pi$ and the hyperbolic <ConceptLink targetId="area">area</ConceptLink> of the triangle equals its <ConceptLink targetId="defecto-angular">defect</ConceptLink>. Hence no triangle has area greater than or equal to $\pi$.
    </PropiedadItem>

    <PropiedadItem
      id="poincare-curvatura"
      title="Constant curvature"
      formula="K = -1"
    >
      With the line element $ds$, the disk is a Riemannian surface of constant Gaussian <ConceptLink targetId="curvatura">curvature</ConceptLink> $-1$; it corresponds to the hyperbolic plane with length constant $k = 1$.
    </PropiedadItem>

    <PropiedadItem
      id="poincare-modelo-espacial"
      title="Spatial model"
      formula="\mathbb{B}^3 = \{ x \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid \lVert x \rVert < 1 \}"

      The disk has no planes and therefore cannot satisfy the spatial axioms <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-incidencia-4">I.4</ConceptLink>–<ConceptLink targetId="axioma-incidencia-8">I.8</ConceptLink>. The Poincaré ball $\mathbb{B}^3$, whose planes are the discs and spherical caps orthogonal to its boundary, is a model of the whole <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-hiperbolico">hyperbolic system</ConceptLink> and, in particular, of the <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-absoluto">absolute system</ConceptLink>.
    </PropiedadItem>
  </PropiedadesGrupo>
