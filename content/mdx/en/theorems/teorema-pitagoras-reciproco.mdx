<Capitular letra="I" />f in a <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo">triangle</ConceptLink> the sum of the squares of the <ConceptLink targetId="longitud">lengths</ConceptLink> of two sides is equal to the square of the length of the third side, then the <ConceptLink targetId="angulo">angle</ConceptLink> opposite that third side is a <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-recto">right angle</ConceptLink>, and the figure is a <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo-rectangulo" isDependency={true}>right triangle</ConceptLink>.

The culminating proposition of Book I of the <ConceptLink targetId="elementos-euclides">Elements</ConceptLink> of <ConceptLink targetId="euclides">Euclid</ConceptLink> (Proposition I.48), this result establishes the exact logical converse of the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-pitagoras" isDependency={true}>Pythagorean theorem</ConceptLink> and provides a necessary and sufficient condition to verify orthogonality without relying on direct angle measurements. The classical synthetic deduction proceeds by constructing an auxiliary right triangle and deducing full congruence via the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-congruencia-lll" isDependency={true}>SSS congruence theorem</ConceptLink>. Depending on the validity of the direct theorem on the auxiliary figure, it belongs to the <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-euclidiano">Euclidean system</ConceptLink> and presupposes <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-euclides" isDependency={true}>Euclid's parallel axiom</ConceptLink>.

<Definicion title="Formal statement of the converse of the Pythagorean theorem">
Let $\triangle ABC$ be a <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo">triangle</ConceptLink> situated in a <ConceptLink targetId="plano">plane</ConceptLink> $\sigma$ of the <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-euclidiano">Euclidean system</ConceptLink>, with <ConceptLink targetId="lado">sides</ConceptLink> of <ConceptLink targetId="longitud">lengths</ConceptLink> $a = |BC|$, $b = |AC|$, and $c = |AB|$ opposite respectively to <ConceptLink targetId="vertice">vertices</ConceptLink> $A$, $B$, and $C$.

If the metric relation is satisfied:

$$
a^2 + b^2 = c^2
$$

or equivalently $|BC|^2 + |AC|^2 = |AB|^2$, then the <ConceptLink targetId="angulo">angle</ConceptLink> $\angle ACB$ is a <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-recto">right angle</ConceptLink>, and $\triangle ABC$ is a <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo-rectangulo">right triangle</ConceptLink> with <ConceptLink targetId="hipotenusa">hypotenuse</ConceptLink> $\overline{AB}$ and <ConceptLink targetId="cateto">legs</ConceptLink> $\overline{BC}$ and $\overline{AC}$.
</Definicion>

<Nota>
Unlike in <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-absoluto">absolute geometry</ConceptLink>, where it is impossible to characterize orthogonality solely through quadratic distance relations without introducing Euclidean parallelism, the Pythagorean converse is intrinsically Euclidean: in the <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-hiperbolico">hyperbolic system</ConceptLink>, a triple with $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ does not produce a right angle, as metric relations are governed by hyperbolic trigonometry.
</Nota>
