<Capitular letra="T" />he area of a rectangle theorem provides the fundamental multiplicative formula for <ConceptLink targetId="teoria-medida-area">surface area measurement</ConceptLink> in Euclidean <ConceptLink targetId="plano">plane geometry</ConceptLink>. It serves as the canonical benchmark from which area formulas for all other polygonal figures are derived.

<Separador />

### Formal statement

Let $R$ be a <ConceptLink targetId="rectangulo">rectangle</ConceptLink> in the Euclidean plane $\mathcal{P}$ whose adjacent <ConceptLink targetId="perpendicular">orthogonal sides</ConceptLink> have lengths $b = \overline{AB}$ (base length) and $h = \overline{AD}$ (height length). The <ConceptLink targetId="area">area</ConceptLink> measure of the rectangular region $R$ strictly satisfies:

<Formula>
  $$ \text{Area}(R) = b \cdot h $$
</Formula>

That is, the area of a rectangular region equals the arithmetic product of the linear measures of its two orthogonal dimensions.

<Separador />

### Proof and extension from rationals to real numbers

The rigorous proof is built in three stages using the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-area-aditividad">area additivity theorem</ConceptLink>:
1. **Unit standard**: The area of the <ConceptLink targetId="cuadrado">unit square</ConceptLink> ($b=1, h=1$) is defined by convention to be $1$.
2. **Rational dimensions**: If $b = \frac{p}{q}$ and $h = \frac{m}{n}$ with $p, q, m, n \in \mathbb{N}$, the rectangle is partitioned into $p \cdot m$ sub-squares of side length $\frac{1}{q \cdot n}$. The additive sum of their areas proves that $\text{Area}(R) = \frac{p \cdot m}{q \cdot n} = b \cdot h$.
3. **Real dimensions**: By the completeness of the real numbers and the <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-arquimedes">Archimedean property</ConceptLink>, the result extends continuously to irrational or incommensurable lengths.

<Separador />

### Role in geometric area theory

- **Foundation for parallelograms**: Every <ConceptLink targetId="paralelogramo">parallelogram</ConceptLink> is equidecomposable with a rectangle sharing the same base and height.
- **Foundation for triangles**: A rectangle is bisected along its diagonal into two <ConceptLink targetId="congruencia">congruent</ConceptLink> triangles, establishing that the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-area-triangulo">area of a triangle</ConceptLink> is $\frac{1}{2} b \cdot h$.
