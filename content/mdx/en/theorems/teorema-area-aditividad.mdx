<Capitular letra="I" />f a <ConceptLink targetId="region-poligonal" isDependency={true}>polygonal region</ConceptLink> is the union of finitely many polygonal regions with pairwise disjoint <ConceptLink targetId="interior-triangulo">interiors</ConceptLink>, its <ConceptLink targetId="teoria-medida-area" isDependency={true}>area</ConceptLink> is the sum of the areas of the pieces.

The definition of an <ConceptLink targetId="teoria-medida-area" isDependency={true}>area function</ConceptLink> only requires additivity for two pieces; this <ConceptLink targetId="teorema">theorem</ConceptLink> extends it to any finite number of pieces, which is how it is used when a rectangle is decomposed into squares, a triangle into two right triangles, or a square into rectangles and triangles. It is the step that allows areas to be computed by cutting figures up and reassembling them.

<Definicion title="Formal statement of the area additivity theorem">
Let $\operatorname{A}$ be an <ConceptLink targetId="teoria-medida-area" isDependency={true}>area function</ConceptLink> on the <ConceptLink targetId="region-poligonal" isDependency={true}>polygonal regions</ConceptLink> of a <ConceptLink targetId="plano" isDependency={true}>plane</ConceptLink> $\sigma$, and let $R_1, \dots, R_n \in \mathcal{R}_\sigma$, with $n \geq 1$, be regions with pairwise disjoint interiors: for $i \neq j$, $R_i \cap R_j$ is contained in a finite union of <ConceptLink targetId="segmento" isDependency={true}>segments</ConceptLink>. If $R = R_1 \cup \dots \cup R_n$, then

$$
\operatorname{A}(R) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \operatorname{A}(R_i)
$$
</Definicion>

<SeccionPropiedades>
  <PropiedadesGrupo title="Consequences">
    <PropiedadItem id="area-regiones-equidescomponibles" title="Equidecomposable regions" formula="P = \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} T_i,\ Q = \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} T'_i,\ T_i \cong T'_i \implies \operatorname{A}(P) = \operatorname{A}(Q)">
      If two <ConceptLink targetId="region-poligonal">polygonal regions</ConceptLink> decompose into triangular regions with disjoint interiors that are pairwise <ConceptLink targetId="congruencia-triangulos">congruent</ConceptLink> (<ConceptLink targetId="equidescomponibilidad">equidecomposability</ConceptLink>), they have the same area: it suffices to add up the areas of the pieces.
    </PropiedadItem>
    <PropiedadItem id="monotonia-area" title="Monotonicity" formula="R' \subseteq R \implies \operatorname{A}(R') \leq \operatorname{A}(R)">
      If $R' \subseteq R$, the region $R$ is the union of $R'$ and of the closure of $R \setminus R'$, which have disjoint interiors; by additivity and positivity of area, $\operatorname{A}(R') \leq \operatorname{A}(R)$.
    </PropiedadItem>
  </PropiedadesGrupo>
</SeccionPropiedades>

<Nota>
The converse of the first consequence holds in the Euclidean plane: two polygonal regions of equal area are equidecomposable (<ConceptLink targetId="teorema-bolyai-gerwien">Bolyai–Gerwien theorem</ConceptLink>). In space it fails: <ConceptLink targetId="dehn">Max Dehn</ConceptLink> (1900) proved that there are <ConceptLink targetId="poliedro">polyhedra</ConceptLink> of equal volume that are not equidecomposable, thereby solving <ConceptLink targetId="tercer-problema-hilbert">Hilbert's third problem</ConceptLink>; Sydler (1965) proved the converse: two polyhedra with equal volume and equal <ConceptLink targetId="invariante-dehn">Dehn invariant</ConceptLink> are equidecomposable.
</Nota>
