<Capitular letra="I" />n every <VisualBind element="triangulo" color="carbon"><ConceptLink targetId="triangulo-rectangulo" isDependency={true}>right triangle</ConceptLink></VisualBind>, the square of the <ConceptLink targetId="longitud" isDependency={true}>length</ConceptLink> of the <ConceptLink targetId="hipotenusa" isDependency={true}>hypotenuse</ConceptLink> is equal to the sum of the squares of the lengths of the <ConceptLink targetId="cateto" isDependency={true}>legs</ConceptLink>. It is a theorem of the <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-euclidiano">Euclidean system</ConceptLink>: it depends on <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-euclides" isDependency={true}>Axiom IV</ConceptLink> and fails in the <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-hiperbolico">hyperbolic system</ConceptLink>.

In terms of <ConceptLink targetId="teoria-medida-area" isDependency={true}>area</ConceptLink>, which is how <ConceptLink targetId="euclides">Euclid</ConceptLink> states it (Elements I.47), the <ConceptLink targetId="cuadrado">square</ConceptLink> constructed on the hypotenuse has the same area as the two squares constructed on the legs combined. The two <ConceptLink targetId="demostracion">proofs</ConceptLink> in this encyclopedia are proofs by area: that of <ConceptLink targetId="demo-pitagoras-euclides">Euclid</ConceptLink> divides the square on the hypotenuse into two rectangles, and that of <ConceptLink targetId="demo-pitagoras-areas">dissection</ConceptLink> compares two decompositions of the same square; both are based on the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-area-triangulo" isDependency={true}>triangle area theorem</ConceptLink>, the <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-area-rectangulo" isDependency={true}>rectangle area theorem</ConceptLink> and <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-area-aditividad" isDependency={true}>area additivity</ConceptLink>. It can also be proved by the <ConceptLink targetId="semejanza">similarity</ConceptLink> of the triangles determined by the altitude to the hypotenuse.

<Definicion title="Formal statement of the Pythagorean theorem">
Let $\triangle ABC$ be a <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo" isDependency={true}>triangle</ConceptLink> in a <ConceptLink targetId="plano" isDependency={true}>plane</ConceptLink> $\sigma$ of the <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-euclidiano">Euclidean system</ConceptLink> with a <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-recto" isDependency={true}>right angle</ConceptLink> at $C$, and let <VisualBind element="cuadrado-a" color="pavo">$a = |BC|$</VisualBind> and <VisualBind element="cuadrado-b" color="terracota">$b = |CA|$</VisualBind> be the <ConceptLink targetId="longitud" isDependency={true}>lengths</ConceptLink> of the <ConceptLink targetId="cateto" isDependency={true}>legs</ConceptLink> and <VisualBind element="cuadrado-c" color="ocre">$c = |AB|$</VisualBind> that of the <ConceptLink targetId="hipotenusa" isDependency={true}>hypotenuse</ConceptLink>. Then

$$
a^2 + b^2 = c^2
$$

Equivalently, if $Q_a$, $Q_b$ and $Q_c$ are the regions of the squares constructed on $\overline{BC}$, $\overline{CA}$ and $\overline{AB}$, then $\operatorname{A}(Q_a) + \operatorname{A}(Q_b) = \operatorname{A}(Q_c)$.
</Definicion>

    <PropiedadItem id="teorema-reciproco-pitagoras" title="Converse theorem" formula="|BC|^2 + |CA|^2 = |AB|^2 \implies \angle ACB \text{ is right}">
      If the <ConceptLink targetId="longitud">lengths</ConceptLink> of the sides of a <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo">triangle</ConceptLink> $\triangle ABC$ satisfy $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, the angle opposite to side $\overline{AB}$ is <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-recto">right</ConceptLink> (Elements I.48; <ConceptLink targetId="teorema-reciproco-pitagoras">converse of the Pythagorean theorem</ConceptLink>). It allows recognizing right angles from <ConceptLink targetId="terna-pitagorica">Pythagorean triples</ConceptLink> such as $3, 4, 5$.
    </PropiedadItem>
    <PropiedadItem id="pitagoras-equivalente-paralelas" title="Equivalence with the parallel axiom">
      Over <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-absoluto">absolute geometry</ConceptLink>, that the theorem holds for every right triangle is equivalent to the <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-euclides">parallel axiom</ConceptLink>: in the <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-hiperbolico">hyperbolic plane</ConceptLink> the relation fails for every right triangle.
    </PropiedadItem>
  </PropiedadesGrupo>
</SeccionPropiedades>

<Nota>
In analytical models of non-Euclidean geometries the relation takes another form. In the normalized hyperbolic plane with curvature $-1$ (for example, the <ConceptLink targetId="modelo-poincare">Poincaré model</ConceptLink>), a right triangle satisfies $\cosh c = \cosh a \cosh b$; on a sphere of radius $1$ (<ConceptLink targetId="geometria-esferica">spherical geometry</ConceptLink>), it satisfies $\cos c = \cos a \cos b$. For small triangles, expanding both formulas up to second order yields $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.
</Nota>
