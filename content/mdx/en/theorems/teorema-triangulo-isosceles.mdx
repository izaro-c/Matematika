<Capitular letra="I" />n any <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo" isDependency={true}>triangle</ConceptLink>, if two <ConceptLink targetId="lado" isDependency={true}>sides</ConceptLink> are mutually <ConceptLink targetId="congruencia" isDependency={true}>congruent</ConceptLink>, the <ConceptLink targetId="angulo" isDependency={true}>angles</ConceptLink> opposite those sides are mutually congruent.

This result, traditionally attributed to <ConceptLink targetId="tales">Thales of Miletus</ConceptLink> and formalized by <ConceptLink targetId="euclides">Euclid</ConceptLink> as Proposition I.5 in the <ConceptLink targetId="elementos-euclides">Elements</ConceptLink> (historically known as the *Pons Asinorum*), constitutes the first foundational <ConceptLink targetId="teorema">theorem</ConceptLink> of angular rigidity and <ConceptLink targetId="simetria">symmetry</ConceptLink> in <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-absoluto">absolute geometry</ConceptLink>. Its derivation is completely independent of the <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-paralelas-euclides">parallel postulate</ConceptLink>, requiring only the <ConceptLink targetId="sistema-incidencia">axioms of incidence</ConceptLink>, <ConceptLink targetId="estar-entre">order</ConceptLink>, and congruence (<ConceptLink targetId="axioma-congruencia-1" isDependency={true}>Axiom III.1</ConceptLink>, <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-congruencia-4" isDependency={true}>Axiom III.4</ConceptLink>, and <ConceptLink targetId="axioma-congruencia-5" isDependency={true}>Axiom III.5</ConceptLink>).

<Definicion title="Formal statement of the isosceles triangle theorem">
Let <VisualBind element="pA" color="terracota">$A$</VisualBind>, <VisualBind element="pB" color="terracota">$B$</VisualBind>, <VisualBind element="pC" color="terracota">$C$</VisualBind> $\in \mathcal{P}$ be three non-<ConceptLink targetId="colinealidad">collinear</ConceptLink> <ConceptLink targetId="punto" isDependency={true}>points</ConceptLink> in a <ConceptLink targetId="plano" isDependency={true}>plane</ConceptLink> $\pi \in \Pi$, determining the <VisualBind element="polyABC" color="carbon"><ConceptLink targetId="triangulo" isDependency={true}>triangle</ConceptLink></VisualBind> $\triangle ABC = \overline{AB} \cup \overline{BC} \cup \overline{CA}$.

If the lateral <ConceptLink targetId="lado" isDependency={true}>side</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="segAB" color="carbon">$\overline{AB}$</VisualBind> is <ConceptLink targetId="congruencia" isDependency={true}>congruent</ConceptLink> to the lateral side <VisualBind element="segAC" color="carbon">$\overline{AC}$</VisualBind>, then the <ConceptLink targetId="angulo-base">base angle</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="anguloB" color="mora">$\angle ABC$</VisualBind> is congruent to the base angle <VisualBind element="anguloC" color="mora">$\angle ACB$</VisualBind>:

$$
\overline{AB} \cong \overline{AC} \implies \angle ABC \cong \angle ACB
$$

The remaining <ConceptLink targetId="segmento" isDependency={true}>segment</ConceptLink> <VisualBind element="segBC" color="carbon">$\overline{BC}$</VisualBind> constitutes the <ConceptLink targetId="base">base</ConceptLink> of the <ConceptLink targetId="triangulo-isosceles" isDependency={true}>isosceles triangle</ConceptLink>, and $\angle BAC$ is the <ConceptLink targetId="angulo">angle</ConceptLink> at the <ConceptLink targetId="vertice">vertex</ConceptLink>.
</Definicion>
